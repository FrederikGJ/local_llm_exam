[SECTION 1: OVERALL ORDER - STEP-BY-STEP GUIDE FOR NEWCOMERS]
Keywords: rækkefølge, trin for trin, tjekliste, hvor starter jeg, første skridt, flytte til Danmark, step by step, checklist, moving to Denmark, getting started, first steps, relocation, newcomer, order of steps, where do I start, to-do list

When a person moves to Denmark from abroad, a number of administrative steps must be completed in a specific order, as the individual systems build on one another:

Step 1: Legal basis for residence (visa, EU registration certificate or residence permit).
Step 2: A fixed home/address in Denmark (a requirement for CPR registration for stays of more than 3 months).
Step 3: Registration with the Civil Registration System (Folkeregistret) and issue of a CPR number (personal identification number) via Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) or International Citizen Service - ICS.
Step 4: Choosing a general practitioner (GP) and issue of the yellow health card (sundhedskort) (happens automatically upon CPR registration).
Step 5: Setting up MitID (the national digital ID, used for public self-service solutions and online banking).
Step 6: Tax card (skattekort) and preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) from the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen).
Step 7: Opening a Danish bank account and linking a NemKonto (the account that receives payments from public authorities).
Step 8: Digital Post (e-Boks / mit.dk / Borger.dk) for communication with public authorities.

1.1 Why the order matters (dependencies between the systems):
- Without a legal basis for residence (opholdsgrundlag), you cannot complete address registration (bopælsregistrering). EU/EEA citizens must therefore have an EU registration certificate from SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration), and third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) must have a residence permit before the municipality can issue a CPR number.
- Without an address in Denmark, you cannot get a CPR number (personal identification number). Housing is therefore often the first practical bottleneck.
- Without a CPR number, you normally cannot get MitID, a Danish bank account, a health card or a regular doctor.
- Without MitID, you cannot log in to skat.dk, borger.dk, sundhed.dk, Digital Post or online banking.
- Without a tax card (skattekort), your employer deducts 55 % tax from your salary.
- Without a bank account and NemKonto, you cannot receive your salary, tax refunds, SU (the Danish state educational grant), housing benefit (boligstøtte) or child benefit (børneydelse) in the normal way.

1.2 Timeline: What can be done before arriving in Denmark?
- Apply for a residence and work permit (third-country nationals) and wait for the decision before booking your journey.
- Find housing, or at least temporary housing where you are allowed to register your address. Get the tenancy agreement (lejekontrakt) or a host declaration (logiværtserklæring) in writing.
- Gather original documents: passport, marriage certificate, children's birth certificates, diplomas and educational certificates, any divorce papers, driving licence. Have them translated and legalised/apostilled before leaving home if they are not in Danish, English, German, Norwegian or Swedish.
- Apply for a tax card from the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) if you already have an employment contract (this can be done without a CPR number, in which case you receive a tax personal number (skattepersonnummer)).
- Take out private health insurance for the first weeks until you are covered by the Danish public health insurance (sygesikring).
- Obtain documentation from your home country that is difficult to get later: tax identification number (TIN), certificate of unemployment insurance (PD U1), claim-free years from your car insurance, children's vaccination records, medical records for chronic illness, and prescriptions.

1.3 Timeline: The first week in Denmark
- EU/EEA citizens: Apply for an EU registration certificate from SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration) or at International Citizen Service (ICS).
- Third-country nationals: Have your biometrics recorded (facial photo and fingerprints) if this has not already been done.
- Book an appointment at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) or ICS for CPR registration, and attend in person with the whole family.
- Put your full name on the letterbox and the door intercom on the same day you move in.
- Choose a general practitioner (GP) when you register for your CPR number.

1.4 Timeline: The first month in Denmark
- Set up MitID (via the app with passport scanning, or in person at Borgerservice).
- Apply for a tax card and fill in the preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) on skat.dk.
- Open a bank account and designate a NemKonto.
- Activate Digital Post and install an app to read it.
- Take out contents insurance (indboforsikring) and liability insurance (ansvarsforsikring), and choose an electricity supplier.
- Register your children for childcare (dagtilbud) or school, and sign up for Danish language classes in your municipality.
- Consider joining an a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) (important to do quickly if insurance periods from another EU/EEA country are to be transferred).

1.5 Timeline: The first 3-6 months in Denmark
- Exchange a foreign driving licence from a country outside the EU/EEA (within 180 days of address registration).
- Register a foreign car you have brought with you (within 30 days).
- Apply for housing benefit (boligstøtte) and child benefit (børne- og ungeydelse) if you meet the conditions.
- Check your preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) again once your first salary has been paid, and correct it if your income differs.
- Order the blue European Health Insurance Card (EU-sygesikringskort) for travel within the EU.

1.6 Typical total time required:
- An EU citizen with a job and housing in place can often have a CPR number, MitID, tax card and bank account within 2-6 weeks.
- For third-country nationals, the processing time for the residence permit comes on top of this (typically 1-3 months before entry, longer for family reunification).

[SECTION 2: CHECKLISTS FOR DIFFERENT TYPES OF NEWCOMERS]
Keywords: tjekliste, scenarie, min situation, EU-borger, arbejdstager, studerende, nordisk statsborger, tredjeland, medfølgende ægtefælle, grænsegænger, pendler, hjemvendt dansker, checklist, EU citizen, worker, student, Nordic citizen, non-EU, accompanying spouse, cross-border commuter, returning Dane, scenario, my situation, third-country national, commuter

2.1 Checklist for EU/EEA citizens moving to Denmark to work:
1) Find housing and get a tenancy agreement or a host declaration (logiværtserklæring). 2) Apply for an EU registration certificate from SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration) as a worker (bring your passport/ID card and an employment contract or employer's declaration). 3) Get a CPR number (personal identification number) at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) or International Citizen Service (ICS). 4) Set up MitID. 5) Apply for a tax card on skat.dk. 6) Open a bank account and NemKonto. 7) Activate Digital Post. 8) Join an a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) within 8 weeks if you want to transfer insurance periods using PD U1. 9) Sign up for free Danish language classes.

2.2 Checklist for EU/EEA citizens moving to Denmark to study:
1) Get a letter of admission from the educational institution. 2) Find student accommodation (a hall of residence (kollegium), youth housing (ungdomsbolig) or a private room). 3) Apply for an EU registration certificate from SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration) as a student (letter of admission and a declaration that you can support yourself). 4) Get a CPR number (personal identification number). 5) Set up MitID and a bank account. 6) If you get a student job: apply for a tax card. 7) Find out whether you can get SU (the Danish state educational grant) as an EU worker (typically requires work of at least 10-12 hours a week alongside your studies).

2.3 Checklist for Nordic citizens (Sweden, Norway, Finland, Iceland):
1) Find housing. 2) Go to Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) in person with your passport or other photo ID and documentation of your housing (no registration certificate or residence permit is needed). 3) Provide your personal identity number from your previous Nordic country of residence so the countries can exchange information. 4) Get a CPR number, MitID, tax card and bank account like everyone else.

2.4 Checklist for third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) with a job in Denmark:
1) The employer and the applicant submit an application for a residence and work permit to SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration), e.g. under the Pay Limit Scheme (Beløbsordningen), the Positive List (Positivlisten) or the Fast-Track Scheme. 2) Pay the fee and have your biometrics recorded. 3) Wait for the permit before starting work (Fast-track can allow a quick start). 4) Enter Denmark and register your address with the municipality to get a CPR number. 5) Set up MitID, a tax card and a bank account. 6) Exchange your driving licence within 180 days. 7) Keep an eye on the permit's expiry date, and apply for an extension in good time (no earlier than 3 months before it expires).

2.5 Checklist for accompanying spouses/partners and children:
1) Apply for a residence permit as an accompanying family member at the same time as the main applicant (third-country nationals) or for an EU registration certificate as a family member (EU citizens). 2) Bring the original marriage certificate and the children's birth certificates, translated and legalised if necessary. 3) The whole family attends CPR registration in person. 4) Put the children's names down for childcare or school. 5) Accompanying spouses of foreign workers can normally work freely in Denmark without their own work permit.

2.6 Checklist for cross-border commuters (grænsegængere/pendlere) living in Sweden, Germany or another country and working in Denmark:
1) You are not to be registered as resident in Denmark. 2) Apply for a tax card from the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) and get a tax personal number (skattepersonnummer, an administrative CPR number). 3) As a general rule, you are covered by social security in the country where you work, Denmark, and can get a special health card. 4) Set up MitID with the administrative CPR number. 5) Open a Danish bank account or register a foreign account as your NemKonto. 6) Look into the cross-border commuter rule (grænsegængerreglen) in the tax system and the double taxation agreement between the countries.

2.7 Checklist for Danish citizens returning home after living abroad:
1) Notify the municipality of your arrival no later than 5 days after moving (can often be done digitally on borger.dk if you have MitID). 2) Your old CPR number is reactivated, and you get a health card and a doctor again. 3) Apply for a tax card/preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse). 4) Check your a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) situation (PD U1 from the country where you lived). 5) A foreign spouse from a third country must apply for family reunification under the Danish rules or the EU rules.

[SECTION 3: LEGAL BASIS FOR RESIDENCE (NORDIC COUNTRIES, EU/EEA AND THIRD COUNTRIES)]
Keywords: opholdstilladelse, arbejdstilladelse, EU-registreringsbevis, registreringsbevis, visum, SIRI, Udlændingestyrelsen, nyidanmark, Beløbsordningen, Positivlisten, Fast-track, familiesammenføring, permanent ophold, statsborgerskab, biometri, opholdskort, residence permit, work permit, EU residence document, registration certificate, visa, Pay Limit Scheme, Positive List, family reunification, permanent residence, citizenship, residence card, Danish Immigration Service, legal basis for residence, biometrics, Fast-Track Scheme, third-country national

The rules for entering and staying in Denmark depend on your citizenship:

3.1 Nordic citizens (Sweden, Norway, Finland, Iceland):
- Nordic citizens can freely enter, live, study and work in Denmark without a visa, residence permit or EU registration certificate.
- For stays of more than 6 months, you must be registered in the Danish Civil Registration System (folkeregister, CPR). For stays of between 3 and 6 months, you can choose to be registered, or you can get an administrative CPR number (tax personal number, skattepersonnummer) if you are working.
- Moving between Nordic countries follows a common Nordic agreement on population registration: You report the move in the country you are moving to, and the authorities themselves notify the country you are moving from.

3.2 EU/EEA citizens and Swiss citizens:
- May stay in Denmark for up to 3 months without registration (up to 6 months if actively seeking work).
- For stays of more than 3 months, you must apply for an EU residence document (registration certificate) from the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration (SIRI). The application must be submitted no later than 3 months after entry.
- Conditions for an EU registration certificate (EU-registreringsbevis): You must meet one of the following criteria: worker, self-employed person, student, person with sufficient resources (self-supporting) or family member of an EU citizen in Denmark.
- Important: The EU registration certificate from SIRI must be issued BEFORE you can complete address registration and get a CPR number at the municipality/Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service).
- Applying for an EU registration certificate is free of charge. You fill in the application online via nyidanmark.dk and then attend in person at SIRI or International Citizen Service (ICS) to show ID.

3.3 Documentation for an EU registration certificate by category:
- Worker: Passport or national ID card and an employment contract or employer's declaration. The work must be genuine and effective (not just a few hours of a purely marginal nature).
- Self-employed: Documentation that the business is established in Denmark or is in the process of being established (e.g. CVR registration (business registration), accounts, contracts with customers).
- Student: Letter of admission from an approved Danish educational institution and a declaration that you can support yourself during your stay.
- Person with sufficient resources: Documentation of funds or income ensuring that you do not become a burden on the public purse (e.g. bank statements or a pension), and normally health insurance.
- Family member of an EU citizen: Documentation of the family relationship (marriage certificate, birth certificate) and of the main person's legal basis for residence. Family members who are themselves third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) receive an EU residence card instead of a registration certificate.

3.4 Rights of EU citizens after registration:
- After 5 years of continuous legal residence in Denmark under EU rules, EU/EEA citizens and their family members have the right to permanent residence (tidsubegrænset ophold) and can obtain proof of this from SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration).
- An EU worker who becomes involuntarily unemployed can, under certain conditions, retain their status as a worker and thereby their right of residence for a period.
- EU citizens have the right to equal treatment with Danish citizens on the labour market and for most social benefits when they have worker status.

3.5 Citizens of countries outside the Nordic countries and the EU/EEA (third-country nationals):
- As a general rule, you must have a residence and work permit (or a residence permit for study or family reasons) issued BEFORE entering Denmark. People who are already staying legally in Denmark (e.g. visa-free or with another permit) can in some cases submit the application here.
- Applications are submitted via Ny i Danmark (nyidanmark.dk) to SIRI or the Danish Immigration Service (Udlændingestyrelsen), depending on the legal basis for residence, e.g. the Pay Limit Scheme (Beløbsordningen), the Positive List (Positivlisten), Fast-track, studies or family reunification.
- SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration) handles cases concerning work, studies, au pairs, internships and accompanying family members of workers and students. The Danish Immigration Service handles, among other things, family reunification, asylum, permanent residence permits and visas.
- After entering Denmark, you must register your address with the municipality to get a CPR number (personal identification number) and receive your residence card by post.
- It is illegal to work in Denmark without a work permit. Both the employee and the employer can be fined, and the foreign national risks expulsion.

3.6 The main work schemes for third-country nationals:
- The Pay Limit Scheme (Beløbsordningen): For people with a job offer with a high annual salary. The pay limit was approx. DKK 514,000 per year in 2025 and is adjusted every year. There are no requirements regarding education or sector.
- The Supplementary Pay Limit Scheme (Supplerende beløbsordning): Lower pay limit (approx. DKK 415,000 in 2025), but with additional conditions, including that the position has been advertised on Jobnet and EURES.
- A special scheme has also been adopted with a lower pay limit (about DKK 300,000) for citizens of a number of selected countries when employed by companies covered by a collective agreement (overenskomst). The exact conditions and the list of countries are available on nyidanmark.dk.
- The Positive List (Positivlisten) for people with a higher education: For jobs in professional fields with a shortage of highly educated labour (e.g. engineers, doctors, IT specialists). The list is updated twice a year (1 January and 1 July).
- The Positive List for skilled work: For jobs in skilled trades with a shortage of labour.
- The Fast-Track Scheme (Fast-track-ordningen): For employees of companies certified by SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration). Provides fast processing and the option to start work before the final permit has been issued. The scheme has several tracks: the pay limit track, the supplementary pay limit track, the researcher track, the education track and the short-term track.
- The Researcher Scheme (Forskerordningen): For researchers employed at universities or research companies. There is no requirement for a specific salary, but the salary and terms must be customary.
- Start-up Denmark: For entrepreneurs from third countries who want to run an innovative growth company in Denmark. The business plan must be approved by an expert panel.
- Other schemes: Trainees, herd managers and farm managers in agriculture, special individual qualifications (e.g. artists, athletes and specialised chefs), and supplementary employment (bibeskæftigelse).
- Common requirements: Salary and employment conditions must match Danish standards, and the salary must normally be paid into a Danish bank account.

3.7 Third-country nationals who want to study in Denmark:
- Requires admission to an approved programme, documentation that you can support yourself and, normally, that the tuition fee for the first semester has been paid.
- Students in higher education may typically work a limited number of hours alongside their studies (about 90 hours per month) and full time in June, July and August.
- After completing a Danish bachelor's, master's or PhD programme, you can get a job-seeking residence permit (jobsøgningsophold) for up to 3 years.
- The rules on work and accompanying family members have been tightened for certain programmes that are not state-approved. Always check the specific programme on nyidanmark.dk.

3.8 Family reunification and accompanying family members:
- Accompanying family members of workers and students (SIRI): A spouse, registered partner or long-term cohabiting partner and children under 18 can get a residence permit if the family lives together and can support itself. The accompanying spouse may work freely.
- Spousal reunification (ægtefællesammenføring) under the Aliens Act (the Danish Immigration Service, Udlændingestyrelsen): Applies when the partner living in Denmark is a Danish citizen or has permanent residence. A number of requirements apply, including the 24-year rule (both must normally be at least 24 years old), a housing requirement, a self-support requirement, an integration requirement, a financial guarantee to the municipality (approx. DKK 57,000 at 2025 level) and passing a Danish test at A1 and A2 level after arrival.
- Family reunification (familiesammenføring) under EU rules: An EU citizen in Denmark, or a Danish citizen who has used the right to free movement in another EU country and returns home, can bring their family to Denmark under the more lenient EU rules (handled by SIRI, the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration).
- Children: As a general rule, children under 15 can be reunified with a parent in Denmark. The application should be submitted as soon as possible after the parent has been granted a residence permit.

3.9 The application process at SIRI and the Danish Immigration Service (third-country nationals):
- Step 1: Create a case order ID on nyidanmark.dk and pay the fee. Fees are adjusted annually and range from approx. DKK 2,000 to over DKK 6,000 for study and work cases and about DKK 10,000 for spousal reunification.
- Step 2: Fill in the digital application (the employer fills in their part in work-related cases) and attach documentation: passport (all pages), employment contract, diplomas etc.
- Step 3: Have your biometrics recorded (facial photo, fingerprints and signature) no later than 14 days after the application has been submitted. This is done at a Danish diplomatic mission or a visa application centre abroad, or at the citizen service of SIRI/the Danish Immigration Service in Denmark.
- Step 4: Wait for the decision. The indicative processing time is approx. 1 month for Fast-track and the Pay Limit Scheme, 1-2 months for studies and several months for family reunification.
- Step 5: The residence card (a plastic card with a chip, in credit card format) is sent to your address in Denmark once you have completed address registration. The residence card is proof of your permit and must be carried together with your passport when travelling.

3.10 Extension, changing jobs and losing your job:
- A residence permit based on work is tied to the specific job. If you change employer, you must normally apply for a new permit no later than the day you start the new job.
- An extension must be applied for before the permit expires, and no earlier than 3 months before expiry. If you apply on time, you may stay in Denmark and continue working while the application is being processed.
- If you lose your job through no fault of your own, under several schemes you can get a job-seeking residence permit for up to 6 months to find new work. SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration) must be notified that the employment has ended.
- Your passport must be valid. A residence permit is normally only granted until 3 months before your passport's expiry date.
- A residence permit can lapse if you give up your residence in Denmark or stay outside the country for more than 6 consecutive months (12 months if you have lived legally in Denmark for more than 2 years).

3.11 Visas and short stays in Schengen:
- Denmark is part of the Schengen cooperation. Third-country nationals may stay in the Schengen area for a maximum of 90 days within a 180-day period, either visa-free (e.g. citizens of the USA, Canada, Japan, Australia and the United Kingdom) or with a Schengen visa.
- A visa or a visa-free stay does not give the right to work in Denmark.
- The EU's Entry/Exit System (EES) registers third-country nationals' biometrics at the Schengen external borders and was introduced gradually from autumn 2025. The ETIAS travel authorisation for visa-exempt travellers is expected to be introduced afterwards.
- A residence permit in Denmark gives the right to visit other Schengen countries for up to 90 days per 180 days, but not to live or work there.

3.12 British citizens after Brexit:
- Since 1 January 2021, British citizens have been third-country nationals and must have a residence and work permit under the general rules to move to Denmark.
- British citizens who lived legally in Denmark before the end of 2020 are covered by the Withdrawal Agreement and have a special residence document.

3.13 Special groups:
- Au pair: For young people (typically aged 18-29) who live with a host family. A maximum of 30 hours of household duties per week, and the host family pays pocket money, board and lodging as well as, among other things, insurance and part of the Danish language classes.
- Working Holiday: Young people from countries including Argentina, Australia, Canada, Chile, Japan, New Zealand and South Korea can get a stay of up to 1 year with a limited right to work.
- Displaced persons from Ukraine: Have access to a temporary residence permit under a special act, which gives the right to work, attend school and receive healthcare services. The special act has been extended several times.
- Asylum seekers and refugees are covered by separate rules under the Danish Immigration Service (Udlændingestyrelsen) and are not covered in further detail in this knowledge base.

3.14 Permanent residence permit (indefinite residence) for third-country nationals:
- Main rule: You can apply after 8 years of legal residence in Denmark. If you meet all four supplementary conditions, you can apply after just 4 years.
- Basic conditions (all must be met): be at least 18 years old, continue to meet the conditions for your current permit, no serious crime, no overdue debt to public authorities, not have received certain public benefits (e.g. social assistance (kontanthjælp)) within the last 4 years, have passed Danish Test 2 (Prøve i Dansk 2), have signed a declaration on integration and active citizenship, and have had ordinary full-time employment for at least 3 years and 6 months within the last 4 years.
- Supplementary conditions: have passed Danish Test 3 (Prøve i Dansk 3), full-time employment for at least 4 years within the last 4 years and 6 months, have passed the citizenship test (medborgerskabsprøve) or shown active citizenship for at least 1 year, and an annual taxable income above a set threshold (approx. DKK 330,000 at 2025 level) in the last 2 years.
- Applications are submitted to the Danish Immigration Service (Udlændingestyrelsen) and a fee is charged.

3.15 Danish citizenship (indfødsret):
- Foreign nationals acquire Danish citizenship through naturalisation, i.e. by being included in a bill on the granting of citizenship, which the Danish Parliament (Folketinget) typically passes twice a year.
- Main requirements: permanent residence permit, normally 9 years of continuous residence in Denmark (shorter for, among others, Nordic citizens, refugees and spouses of Danish citizens), passed Danish Test 3 (Prøve i Dansk 3), passed the citizenship test (Indfødsretsprøve), self-sufficiency, no debt to public authorities and no serious crime.
- The application costs approx. DKK 6,000. Finally, you must take part in a constitutional ceremony (grundlovsceremoni) in your municipality, where you sign a declaration and shake hands.
- Denmark allows dual citizenship, so you do not have to give up your original citizenship under Danish rules.
- Children automatically acquire Danish citizenship at birth if at least one of the parents is a Danish citizen. Being born in Denmark does not in itself give Danish citizenship.

[SECTION 4: CPR NUMBER (THE CENTRAL PERSON REGISTER) AND THE CIVIL REGISTRATION SYSTEM (FOLKEREGISTER)]
Keywords: CPR, CPR-nummer, personnummer, folkeregister, bopælsregistrering, Borgerservice, ICS, International Citizen Service, logiværtserklæring, bopælsattest, flytteanmeldelse, adressebeskyttelse, skattepersonnummer, civil registration number, personal identification number, CPR number, register address, national register, proof of address, landlord declaration, change of address, administrative CPR, host declaration, certificate of residence, address registration, address protection, moving within Denmark

What is a CPR number?
- A 10-digit personal identification number (DDMMYY-XXXX) that identifies every person who is resident or liable to tax in Denmark.
- The first 6 digits are the date of birth, and the last 4 digits are a serial number (the last digit indicates legal gender: odd for men, even for women).
- The CPR number is the key to Danish society: healthcare, banking, tax, salary payments, education, libraries and insurance.
- You keep the same CPR number for life, even if you move away from Denmark and later return.
- The CPR number is not secret, but it is confidential. It should only be given to authorities, your employer, bank, doctor and others with a legitimate need, and it should not be sent in unencrypted emails or shared on social media.

4.1 When must you register your address and get a CPR number?
- If you move to Denmark to live here for more than 3 months (for Nordic citizens: more than 6 months).
- EU/EEA citizens and Swiss citizens have the right to registration for stays of more than 3 months and can in practice wait if the stay lasts less than 6 months, but most people need the CPR number (personal identification number) straight away.
- Notification of arrival must be made no later than 5 days after the conditions are met (i.e. you have entered the country, have a legal basis for residence and have a fixed home).

4.2 The three conditions for getting a CPR number when moving to Denmark:
- Condition 1: Your stay in Denmark must last more than 3 months.
- Condition 2: You must have legal residence (Nordic citizenship, EU registration certificate or residence permit).
- Condition 3: You must have a home or a fixed place of residence in Denmark.
- The municipality can refuse the registration if one of the conditions has not been documented. The decision can be appealed.

4.3 Housing/address requirements for CPR registration:
- You must have a fixed home or a fixed place of residence in Denmark where you actually live and sleep.
- A PO box or a c/o address without actual residence is not valid.
- Documentation of housing: A signed tenancy agreement (lejekontrakt), a title deed to a home, or a host declaration (logiværtserklæring) (if you rent a room from/live with another person who is named on the tenancy agreement or owns the home).
- A host declaration is a signed declaration from the person you live with stating that you actually live at the address. Many municipalities have a form for this, and the host can often confirm digitally with MitID.
- Subletting (fremleje): If you live in a sublet, the sublease agreement must be valid, and in some cases the landlord or the housing association must have approved the subletting.
- You can only have one registered address in the Civil Registration System (folkeregisteradresse). It is a criminal offence to register at an address where you do not live (a sham address).

4.4 Required documentation when attending in person (Borgerservice / ICS):
You must attend in person (often by appointment) and bring original documents:
- Valid passport or national ID card (for EU/EEA citizens).
- EU registration certificate (from SIRI) or residence permit (from SIRI/the Danish Immigration Service) – does not apply to Nordic citizens.
- Documentation of residence in Denmark (tenancy agreement, title deed or host declaration).
- Marriage certificate (if you are married, even if your spouse is not moving with you).
- Birth or baptism certificate (particularly relevant for accompanying children under 18, and for documenting parental responsibility).
- Any divorce or death certificate (if relevant to your marital status).
- Note on documents: Documents in Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, English or German are normally accepted without translation. Documents in other languages must be translated by an authorised translator and possibly legalised/bear an Apostille stamp.
- If you have previously lived in another Nordic country, you must provide your previous personal identity number/identification number from that country.

4.5 How CPR registration works, step by step:
- Step 1: Many municipalities ask you to first fill in a digital notification of arrival ("Flytning til Danmark", i.e. Moving to Denmark) on the municipality's website and upload documents.
- Step 2: Book an appointment at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) or International Citizen Service (ICS). In the large cities there may be waiting times, especially in August and September when many international students arrive.
- Step 3: All family members who are to be registered attend in person, including children.
- Step 4: The municipality checks the documents and creates the CPR number (personal identification number). You normally get the number on paper immediately or within a few days.
- Step 5: You choose a doctor. The health card is then sent by post to your address.
- Registration is free of charge.

4.6 International Citizen Service (ICS):
- Foreign nationals moving to Denmark to work or study can have their EU registration, CPR number, health card and tax card sorted in one place at International Citizen Service (ICS) in Copenhagen, Aarhus, Odense or Aalborg.
- ICS is a collaboration between SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration), the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen), the municipalities and Workindenmark. In Copenhagen, ICS is located in International House Copenhagen.
- ICS can normally only be used by citizens who live in or are moving to the municipalities served by the ICS centre in question. If you live elsewhere, you use SIRI's branches and your own municipality's Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service).
- ICS also provides guidance on job seeking, Danish language classes and everyday life in Denmark.

4.7 Administrative CPR number (tax personal number / skattepersonnummer):
- If you work in Denmark for less than 3 months (or are a cross-border commuter living in Sweden/Germany and working in Denmark), you are not to be registered as resident in the Civil Registration System (Folkeregistret).
- Instead, the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) issues an administrative CPR number (tax personal number, skattepersonnummer) in connection with your application for a tax card.
- An administrative CPR number looks like an ordinary CPR number, but does not in itself give the right to a health card, a regular doctor or other rights linked to residence in Denmark.
- If you later take up residence in Denmark, you keep the same number, which then becomes an ordinary CPR number upon address registration.

4.8 Moving within Denmark (change of address):
- A move must be reported to the municipality no later than 5 days after moving. It can be reported no earlier than 4 weeks before the moving date.
- You report the move digitally with MitID on borger.dk ("Flytning"). The whole household can be included in the same notification.
- If you move to another municipality or to an address far from your current doctor, you must choose a new doctor, and you get a new health card free of charge.
- The change of address is automatically passed on to most public authorities, banks and insurance companies, but not necessarily to your employer, subscriptions or foreign authorities.
- Reporting a move late can result in a fine.

4.9 Departure and longer stays abroad:
- You must report your departure to the municipality if you move abroad or stay abroad for more than 6 months.
- Once you are registered as having left the country, you lose your health card and your right to a regular doctor in Denmark, but keep your CPR number and your MitID.
- For third-country nationals, longer stays abroad can mean that the residence permit lapses (see 3.10).

4.10 Certificate of residence and personal certificate:
- A certificate of residence (bopælsattest) is an official document with information about current and previous addresses in Denmark. It is ordered on borger.dk or at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service), normally for a small fee, and is available in English.
- A personal certificate (personattest) contains information from the civil register (name, birth, parents, marital status) and is ordered on borger.dk or from the parish.
- Banks, foreign authorities and employers often ask for a certificate of residence as documentation of address (proof of address).

4.11 Protection of name, address and information in the CPR register:
- Name and address protection (navne- og adressebeskyttelse, a "secret address"): You can ask the municipality not to disclose your name and address to private parties. The protection normally applies for 1 year at a time and is ordered on borger.dk.
- Advertising protection (Robinsonlisten, the Robinson List): You can opt out of addressed advertising and approaches from companies.
- Research protection has been abolished for new registrations, and as a general rule, information can be used for statistics and research.

4.12 Name and marital status in the CPR register:
- As a general rule, your name is registered as it appears in your passport. Letters that do not exist in the Danish alphabet may be transliterated.
- Foreign naming customs (e.g. multiple surnames or patronymics) can cause problems in IT systems with limited field length. Check the spelling at registration, because errors carry over to the health card, bank and MitID.
- Marital status (married, unmarried, divorced, widow/widower) is registered on the basis of the documents you present. Without documentation, marital status is registered as "unknown" ("uoplyst"), which can later cause problems with, for example, marriage, child benefits and inheritance.
- A change of name is applied for digitally on borger.dk and costs a fee, unless the change is made in connection with a wedding.

4.13 Children and CPR numbers:
- Children born in Denmark to a mother who is registered as resident automatically get a CPR number at birth.
- Children who move to Denmark with their parents are registered together with the parents and must attend in person with a passport and birth certificate.
- If only one parent moves with the child, the municipality can ask for consent from the other parent or documentation of parental responsibility (custody).

4.14 What is the CPR number used for in everyday life?
- Doctor's visits, hospital, pharmacy and dentist (the health card is scanned).
- Employment and salary (the employer reports salary and tax under your CPR number).
- Banking, loans, insurance, mobile phone subscription and internet subscription.
- Education, library, day-care facility and school.
- Buying a home or a car, and getting married.
- All digital self-service solutions via MitID.

[SECTION 5: HEALTH CARD, CHOOSING A DOCTOR AND THE HEALTHCARE SYSTEM]
Keywords: sundhedskort, gult kort, sygesikring, sygesikringsbevis, læge, praktiserende læge, lægevagt, 1813, akut, skadestue, akutmodtagelse, 112, tandlæge, apotek, medicin, recept, psykolog, graviditet, jordemoder, vaccination, hospital, speciallæge, blåt kort, EU-sygesikringskort, tolk, health card, yellow card, health insurance, GP, doctor, general practitioner, emergency, dentist, pharmacy, prescription, pregnancy, midwife, hospital, specialist, EHIC, interpreter, out-of-hours medical service, emergency medical helpline, emergency department, European Health Insurance Card

5.1 The yellow health card (sygesikringsbevis):
- When you are registered at an address (bopælsregistrering) in the Civil Registration System (Folkeregistret) and receive a CPR number (personal identification number), you are automatically covered by the Danish public health insurance (insurance group 1 - sikringsgruppe 1 - by default).
- The physical yellow health card is sent by post to your registered address within approx. 2-3 weeks. It is important that your name is clearly displayed on your letterbox; otherwise the letter is returned to the municipality.
- You can also get the digital health card via the app "Sundhedskortet" as soon as you have MitID. The app is just as valid as the physical card.
- The health card entitles you to free treatment from a general practitioner (GP), specialists (after referral) and at public hospitals in Denmark.
- The health card shows your name, address and CPR number as well as your doctor's name, address and telephone number.
- There is no waiting period: you are covered from the day you are registered at your address. While waiting for the card, you can use the letter from the municipality or the app as documentation.
- A lost or damaged health card can be reordered on borger.dk for a fee. When you move, you automatically receive a new card free of charge.
- There are political plans to make the digital health card the standard and to give less prominence to the physical plastic card.

5.2 Insurance group 1 and insurance group 2 (sikringsgruppe 1 and 2):
- Insurance group 1 (the vast majority): You have a regular general practitioner (GP), treatment is free, and you need a referral from your doctor to see most specialists.
- Insurance group 2: You can freely choose a doctor and specialist without a referral, but you pay part of the bill yourself. You change group on borger.dk.

5.3 Choosing a doctor:
- In connection with your CPR registration, you choose a general practitioner (GP, praktiserende læge, general medicine) from among the doctors in your local area who are accepting new patients.
- Later changes of doctor can be made digitally via borger.dk for a fee (approx. DKK 230-250), unless you move address.
- Children under 15 normally have the same doctor as their parents.
- The GP is the gateway to the rest of the healthcare system and is often called "your own doctor" (egen læge). The doctor treats most things themselves and refers you on to a specialist, hospital, psychologist or physiotherapist.

5.4 How to use your general practitioner (GP):
- Appointments are booked online via the doctor's website or the app "Min Læge", or by telephone, typically in the morning between 8:00 and 9:00.
- The doctor's surgery is normally open on weekdays from 8:00 to 16:00. Most doctors have one evening consultation a week.
- E-consultation: You can send short, non-urgent questions to your doctor digitally and renew prescriptions.
- Failing to attend without cancelling may incur a fee with some practitioners.
- Many doctors speak English. If you need an interpreter, the doctor books the interpreter.

5.5 Acute illness outside your doctor's opening hours (out-of-hours medical service and emergency medical helpline / lægevagt and akuttelefon):
- In case of life-threatening illness or a serious accident, call 112 (ambulance, fire, police).
- For acute but not life-threatening illness outside your doctor's opening hours, call the out-of-hours medical service/emergency medical helpline in your region. You must always call first and must not turn up at the emergency department (akutmodtagelse) without an appointment.
- Region Hovedstaden (Capital Region of Denmark): Emergency medical helpline (Akuttelefonen) 1813.
- Region Sjælland (Region Zealand): Emergency medical helpline (Akuttelefonen) 1818.
- Region Syddanmark (Region of Southern Denmark): Out-of-hours medical service (Lægevagten) 70 11 07 07.
- Region Midtjylland (Central Denmark Region): Out-of-hours medical service (Lægevagten) 70 11 31 31.
- Region Nordjylland (North Denmark Region): Out-of-hours medical service (Lægevagten) 70 15 03 00.
- The hospital's emergency department (akutmodtagelse/skadestue) treats acute injuries and illness. Treatment is free for people with a health card.
- The Poison Information Line (Giftlinjen, 82 12 12 12) provides advice on poisoning around the clock.

5.6 Specialists and hospitals:
- Specialists (e.g. dermatologist, gynaecologist, psychiatrist) normally require a referral (henvisning) from your own doctor. Eye specialists and ear, nose and throat (ENT) specialists can be contacted without a referral.
- Treatment at public hospitals is free. You have a free choice of hospital (frit sygehusvalg) among public hospitals throughout the country.
- Right to rapid diagnostic assessment: You have the right to be assessed (udredning) within 30 days, if this is professionally possible.
- Extended free choice of hospital (udvidet frit sygehusvalg): If the waiting time for treatment in the public system exceeds a set limit (typically 30 days), you can choose a private hospital that has an agreement with the regions, at no cost to yourself.

5.7 Dentist:
- Children and young people receive free municipal dental care. The scheme has been extended so that it gradually covers young people up to and including the age of 21.
- Adults go to a private dentist and pay most of the cost themselves. The public health insurance provides a small subsidy, which is automatically deducted from the bill.
- A standard check-up with cleaning typically costs a few hundred kroner, whereas crowns, root canal treatments and implants are expensive. Prices vary, and dentists must have a price list.

5.8 Medicine, pharmacies and prescriptions:
- Prescription (recept) medicine is prescribed digitally by the doctor and can be collected at any pharmacy (apotek) by showing your health card. Prescriptions can be viewed on "Medicinkortet" (your medication record) on sundhed.dk or in the app "Medicinkortet".
- Medicine subsidy (tilskud): The public sector automatically provides a subsidy that increases with your total annual spending on medicine eligible for subsidy. Adults pay the first approx. DKK 1,100 a year themselves; after that, an increasing share is covered, and there is a ceiling on the annual co-payment.
- Over-the-counter medicine (e.g. painkillers) can be bought at pharmacies and, to a limited extent, in supermarkets.
- Many types of medicine that are sold freely in other countries (e.g. antibiotics) require a prescription in Denmark.
- Medicine brought from abroad: Bring a prescription or a doctor's statement, and have your Danish doctor assess and prescribe equivalent medicine.

5.9 Psychologists, psychiatry and mental health:
- Your own doctor (GP) can refer you to a psychologist with a public subsidy (approx. 60 % of the fee) for specific reasons, e.g. depression, anxiety, serious illness, death in the close family or abuse.
- Young people aged 18-24 can receive free psychological help for mild to moderate depression or anxiety after referral.
- Without a referral, you pay the full price yourself (typically DKK 1,000-1,400 per session).
- Psychiatric emergency departments exist in all regions and can be contacted in an acute mental health crisis.
- Livslinien (70 201 201) offers anonymous counselling for people with suicidal thoughts.

5.10 Pregnancy, childbirth and young children:
- Pregnancy check-ups with your own doctor and midwife (jordemoder), scans, birth in hospital and post-natal visits are free.
- The first step is to contact your own doctor, who fills in a pregnancy record (vandrejournal) and refers you to a midwife and a place of birth.
- After the birth, a health visitor (sundhedsplejerske) from the municipality makes several home visits during the child's first year.
- Children are offered free preventive child health examinations with their own doctor and free vaccinations under the childhood vaccination programme.
- Children who have been vaccinated abroad should bring their vaccination card to the doctor so that the programme can be adjusted.

5.11 Other practitioners:
- Physiotherapy: Subsidy after referral from a doctor (you pay approx. 60 % yourself).
- Chiropractor: Subsidy without referral.
- Glasses and contact lenses are, as a rule, paid for by yourself. An eye test at an optician is often free or cheap.
- Hearing aids: Free via the public system after referral, or a subsidy for private purchase.

5.12 The blue European Health Insurance Card (EU-sygesikringskort):
- When you are socially insured in Denmark (e.g. through work or residence), you can order the blue European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) on borger.dk, which covers necessary medical and hospital treatment during temporary stays in other EU/EEA countries and Switzerland.
- The card is free, is valid for up to 5 years and is issued by Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark).
- The blue card provides cover on the same terms as for citizens of the country you are staying in, so there may be a co-payment. It does not cover transport home.
- The yellow health card no longer provides cover when travelling abroad. Private travel insurance is recommended for all trips, including within the EU.

5.13 Health coverage for those who do not yet have a CPR number, and for tourists:
- Everyone staying in Denmark is entitled to emergency hospital treatment. People who are not resident in Denmark may be charged.
- EU citizens on a temporary stay use the blue European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) from their home country.
- Third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) should have private health insurance until they are registered at an address (bopælsregistrering) in Denmark.
- Cross-border commuters (grænsegængere) who work in Denmark and live in another EU country can get a special health card that gives access to treatment in Denmark.

5.14 Interpreters in the healthcare system:
- The place of treatment books an interpreter if the doctor considers it necessary for the treatment.
- There have been rules on a fee for interpreting assistance for people who have lived in Denmark for more than 3 years. Ask the place of treatment about the current rules.

5.15 Private health insurance:
- Many employers offer private health insurance that gives faster access to e.g. physiotherapy, psychologists and private hospitals.
- "Sygeforsikringen danmark" is a member-owned insurance that gives subsidies for, among other things, dental treatment, glasses and medicine. It is not part of the public health insurance.

5.16 Digital health services:
- sundhed.dk: Your own hospital records, laboratory results, referrals, medication record and vaccinations (requires MitID).
- The app "MinSundhed": Combined access to health information and appointments.
- The app "Min Læge": Appointment booking, e-consultation and video consultation with your own doctor.
- The app "Medicinkortet": Prescriptions and prescription renewal.
- The app "Sundhedskortet": Digital health card.
- Parents have access to their children's information on sundhed.dk until the child turns 15.

5.17 The role of the regions:
- Denmark has 5 regions, which run the hospitals and pay the general practitioners. From 2027, Region Hovedstaden and Region Sjælland will be merged into Region Østdanmark (Region East Denmark) as part of a health reform.
- The 98 municipalities are responsible for, among other things, health visitor services, home care, children's dental care, rehabilitation and prevention.

[SECTION 6: MITID (DIGITAL IDENTITY)]
Keywords: MitID, digitalt ID, login, NemID, MitID-app, kodeviser, kodeoplæser, pas-scanning, vidne, vitterlighedsvidne, aktiveringskode, spærre MitID, MitID Erhverv, digital fuldmagt, digital ID, e-ID, digital signature, code display, passport scan, witness, activation code, block MitID, power of attorney, digital identity, audio code reader

What is MitID?
- MitID is Denmark's national digital ID. It is used to log in to public self-service solutions (borger.dk, skat.dk, sundhed.dk, Digital Post) and online banking, and to sign digital documents (e.g. tenancy agreements and employment contracts).
- MitID has replaced NemID, which has been shut down.
- MitID is personal. You must never share your user ID, your password or your codes with others, not even your spouse or family.
- MitID can be obtained by people aged 13 and over.

6.1 How to get MitID as a newcomer:
- Prerequisite: You must have a CPR number (personal identification number) or an administrative CPR number.
- Method 1 (self-service via the MitID app): If you have a valid passport with a built-in chip (a biometric passport from Denmark or from one of a number of selected countries) and a smartphone with an NFC reader, you can scan your passport and your face in the MitID app and create MitID from home.
- Method 2 (in person at Borgerservice): If your passport is not supported in the app, or you do not have a passport with a chip, you must book an appointment with Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service).
- Requirements when attending Borgerservice in person for MitID:
  * Valid passport or national ID card.
  * A witness (vitterlighedsvidne) who can confirm your identity. The witness must have had an active MitID for at least 30 days, be over 18 and attend in person together with the applicant, bringing their own passport/driving licence.
  * If you do not have a witness, Borgerservice can in some cases verify your identity via the police register (if you have a residence permit/EU registration certificate issued by SIRI with biometrics) or by asking control questions based on information in the Danish CPR register (this typically requires that you have been registered in the CPR for a certain period).
- Getting MitID is free.

6.2 MitID authenticators (identifikationsmidler - ways to approve):
- MitID app: The most widely used solution. You approve a login by opening the app and swiping, entering a PIN code or using fingerprint/facial recognition. The app requires a newer smartphone or tablet.
- MitID code display (kodeviser): A small electronic device that shows a one-time code. Used by people without a smartphone or as extra security in case your phone breaks.
- MitID audio code reader (kodeoplæser): A device that reads the code aloud, for people with visual impairments.
- MitID chip: A physical security key (FIDO) that provides extra high security.
- It is recommended to have an extra authenticator (e.g. a code display or the app on two devices) so that you do not lose access if your phone is lost.

6.3 MitID user ID and password:
- When you create your MitID, you choose a user ID. It must not be your CPR number and should not be easy to guess.
- The user ID is not secret in the same way as a password, but should be kept to yourself, because others can temporarily block your MitID by entering incorrect details.
- If you have forgotten your user ID, you can find it in the MitID app.

6.4 If your phone is lost or replaced (MitID):
- New phone: You can move the MitID app to the new phone using the old phone, a code display or by scanning your passport again.
- Lost phone: Block the MitID app immediately via mitid.dk or by calling MitID support (33 98 00 10, blocking available 24 hours a day).
- If you have no other authenticators and a passport that cannot be scanned, you must attend Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) in person again.

6.5 Security and fraud involving MitID:
- Banks, the police and authorities never ask you to disclose your MitID codes or to approve anything in the app while you are talking to them on the phone.
- Only approve requests in the MitID app that you have initiated yourself, and always read the text in the app, which tells you what you are approving.
- Suspected misuse: Block your MitID, contact your bank and report the matter to the police.
- Typical types of fraud targeting newcomers are fake text messages and emails from "Skat", "MitID", "PostNord" or "the bank" containing links.

6.6 MitID without a CPR number:
- It is technically possible to get a MitID without a Danish CPR number (personal identification number), but it does not give access to the public services that require a CPR number. For newcomers, it is therefore normally necessary to wait for the CPR number.

6.7 MitID Erhverv and powers of attorney:
- MitID Erhverv (MitID for businesses) is used when you act on behalf of a company or association (e.g. on virk.dk and skat.dk).
- Digital power of attorney (fuldmagt): On borger.dk you can give another person power of attorney to act on your behalf in specific public self-service solutions. That person then uses their own MitID.
- You can also give another person read access to your Digital Post.

6.8 Using MitID in other EU countries and foreign e-IDs in Denmark:
- The EU's eIDAS rules mean that some Danish public services can be accessed with an approved digital ID from another EU country. However, this does not cover everything, and banks normally require MitID.

[SECTION 7: DIGITAL POST AND COMMUNICATION WITH PUBLIC AUTHORITIES]
Keywords: Digital Post, e-Boks, mit.dk, borger.dk, post fra det offentlige, fritagelse, læseadgang, NemSMS, brev fra kommunen, digital mail, official mail, letters from authorities, exemption, e-Boks app, public digital mailbox, read access, notifications

7.1 What is Digital Post?
- In Denmark, communication from public authorities (municipality, region, state, hospitals, Skat, police) is digital by default.
- When you receive a CPR number and have turned 15, you are automatically signed up for Digital Post.
- You must activate your Digital Post mailbox yourself and check it regularly, as letters in Digital Post have the same legal effect as physical letters (e.g. hospital appointment notices, tax assessments and decisions).
- A letter in Digital Post is considered to have been delivered once it is in your mailbox. Deadlines (e.g. deadlines for complaints and payments) run regardless of whether you have opened the letter.

7.2 Where do you read Digital Post?
- Digital Post can be read on borger.dk, mit.dk or e-boks.dk (and via the associated mobile apps) using MitID.
- The same public mail is shown in all three places. You choose which solution you want to use.
- e-Boks and mit.dk also show mail from private companies (bank, insurance, payslips, pension company) if you have signed up for them.
- Many employers send payslips to e-Boks.

7.3 Digital Post notifications:
- You can register your email address and mobile number so that you are notified when there is new mail. This is done in the settings the first time you log in.
- NemSMS is a voluntary scheme where authorities can send text message reminders, e.g. about hospital appointments.

7.4 Exemption from Digital Post:
- Exemption: Only people with special challenges (e.g. physical/cognitive disability, language difficulties, lack of access to a computer/internet at home or permanent departure from Denmark) can apply for exemption from Digital Post by attending Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) in person.
- If you are exempt, you receive your mail as physical letters instead.

7.5 Read access to Digital Post for others:
- You can give a spouse, a family member or another person read access to all or part of your Digital Post. That person logs in with their own MitID.

7.6 Digital Post after leaving Denmark:
- The mailbox still exists after you have moved away from Denmark, and authorities can continue to send mail to it (e.g. the annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse) and pension letters). You should therefore keep your MitID and continue to check your mail.

7.7 Help with digital services:
- Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) and the libraries help with using borger.dk, MitID and Digital Post.
- Den Digitale Hotline (the Digital Hotline, 70 20 00 00) provides telephone help with public self-service solutions.
- Much of the content on borger.dk is available in English on lifeindenmark.dk.

[SECTION 8: TAX, TAX CARD, PRELIMINARY INCOME ASSESSMENT AND ANNUAL TAX ASSESSMENT]
Keywords: skat, skattekort, hovedkort, bikort, frikort, forskudsopgørelse, årsopgørelse, trækprocent, fradrag, personfradrag, AM-bidrag, arbejdsmarkedsbidrag, kommuneskat, topskat, restskat, overskydende skat, TastSelv, Skattestyrelsen, forskerskatteordningen, dobbeltbeskatning, tax, tax card, preliminary income assessment, annual tax assessment, tax return, withholding rate, deductions, personal allowance, labour market contribution, tax refund, researcher tax scheme, expat tax, double taxation, main tax card, secondary tax card, tax-free card, back tax, payslip

8.1 Tax liability in Denmark:
- Full tax liability: Normally begins on the day you take up residence in Denmark and start staying here (or after 6 months of continuous stay without a permanent residence). You are liable to tax on your worldwide income (both Danish and foreign income).
- Limited tax liability: Applies to people who live abroad but work in Denmark (e.g. commuters/cross-border commuters). You are only taxed on income earned in Denmark.
- Tax liability is determined by the tax rules and not by whether you have a CPR number or a residence permit.
- If you have ties to two countries, the double taxation agreement between the countries determines where you are tax resident and which country may tax which income.

8.2 Tax card and preliminary income assessment:
- A Danish employer cannot pay your salary correctly without a digital tax card (skattekort). Without a tax card, 55 % withholding tax is automatically deducted.
- How to get a tax card: You apply online to the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) via skat.dk for a tax card and fill in your preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse): your expected income for the rest of the calendar year plus any deductions, such as transport deduction (kørselsfradrag) or interest expenses.
- The first time, you use the digital form "Ansøgning om skattekort/personnummer" (Application for tax card/personal number, form 04.063) on skat.dk. The form can be completed without MitID.
- When applying, you must attach your employment contract, passport/ID and residence documentation. Married people also attach their marriage certificate.
- Once the Danish Tax Agency has processed the application, the tax card is transferred automatically and digitally to your employer (eIndkomst). You do not need to hand anything in to your employer yourself.
- The processing time is typically a few weeks. Apply as early as possible, therefore, preferably before your first salary payment.

8.3 Main tax card, secondary tax card and tax-free card (hovedkort, bikort and frikort):
- Main tax card (hovedkort): Used by your primary employer. It contains the withholding rate (trækprocent) and the monthly deduction (the part of your salary on which no tax is withheld).
- Secondary tax card (bikort): Used by other employers or payers. It has the same withholding rate but no deduction.
- Tax-free card (frikort): Used if your total annual income is expected to be below the personal allowance (approx. DKK 54,100 in 2026). As long as you earn less than the amount on the tax-free card, you only pay labour market contribution (AM-bidrag).
- The employer retrieves the correct card itself. If you have several jobs, you can choose on skat.dk which employer should use the main tax card.

8.4 The preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) in practice:
- The preliminary income assessment is your "tax budget" for the year: expected salary, other income, interest expenses, deductions and pension contributions.
- It can be changed at any time during the year on skat.dk (TastSelv) with MitID. The change takes effect from the next salary payment.
- The preliminary income assessment for the coming year becomes available in November.
- Newcomers who only work part of the year should state the actual income for the period in Denmark. If you are only liable to tax for part of the year, your income is, as a rule, converted to a full-year income (helårsomregning), so that the personal allowance and thresholds are in effect only granted proportionally for the period in which you have been liable to tax.
- An incorrect preliminary income assessment results in either back tax (restskat - you have paid too little) or a tax refund (overskydende skat - you have paid too much).

8.5 How Danish income tax is structured (approx. 2026 level):
- Labour market contribution (AM-bidrag): 8 % of gross salary. Deducted first, before other tax is calculated.
- Personal allowance (personfradrag): Approx. DKK 54,100 a year for people over 18. No income tax is paid on this amount.
- Bottom-bracket tax (bundskat, paid to the state): Approx. 12 %.
- Municipal tax (kommuneskat): Approx. 23-27 % depending on the municipality (average approx. 25 %).
- Church tax (kirkeskat): Approx. 0.4-1.3 %, but only paid by members of the Church of Denmark (Folkekirken). Newcomers are not automatically members.
- Middle-bracket tax (mellemskat): 7.5 % of income above approx. DKK 641,000 after AM-bidrag.
- Top tax (topskat): A further 7.5 % of income above approx. DKK 778,000 after AM-bidrag.
- Top-top tax (toptopskat): A further 5 % of income above approx. DKK 2.6 million after AM-bidrag.
- In 2026, middle-bracket tax, top tax and top-top tax replaced the previous top tax of 15 %.
- The employment deduction (beskæftigelsesfradrag) and job deduction (jobfradrag) are granted automatically to people with wage income and reduce the tax.
- Rule of thumb: An ordinary employee pays a total of around 35-40 % of their salary in tax, including AM-bidrag. The marginal tax rate for the highest incomes is approx. 56-60 %.

8.6 The payslip (lønseddel) explained:
- Gross salary (bruttoløn): Salary before tax.
- ATP contribution: A small statutory pension contribution (approx. DKK 99 a month for a full-time employee; the employer pays twice as much).
- Pension: The employee's own contribution to the occupational (labour market) pension is deducted before tax.
- Labour market contribution (AM-bidrag): 8 % of salary after ATP and your own pension contribution.
- A-tax (A-skat): The withholding rate multiplied by the salary after AM-bidrag and deductions.
- Net salary (nettoløn): The amount paid into your account.
- Holiday pay or holiday supplement, employee benefits (e.g. free phone, company car) and any free-choice account (fritvalgskonto) also appear on the payslip.

8.7 The most common tax deductions (fradrag):
- Transport deduction (befordringsfradrag/kørselsfradrag): Granted if the total distance between home and work is more than 24 km per day, regardless of means of transport. You report the number of working days and the addresses yourself.
- Trade union membership fee: Deductible up to an annual ceiling (approx. DKK 7,000). Reported automatically by the trade union.
- A-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) membership fee: Fully deductible. Reported automatically.
- Interest expenses: Interest on mortgages, bank loans and student loans is deductible. Danish banks report automatically; you must declare foreign interest expenses yourself.
- Pension contributions: Contributions to instalment pensions (ratepension) and life annuities (livrente) are deductible within certain limits.
- Service deduction (servicefradrag): Deduction for certain household services such as cleaning and childcare.
- Gifts to approved charitable organisations: Deductible up to an annual ceiling.
- Travel deduction and deduction for double housekeeping (dobbelt husførelse): May be relevant for temporary work far from your usual residence.
- Maintenance payments for children or a former spouse may be deductible.

8.8 Annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse):
- Every year in March, the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) produces an annual tax assessment for the previous calendar year on skat.dk (TastSelv). It shows whether you have paid too much tax (the tax refund is paid into your NemKonto) or too little (back tax must be paid).
- You are obliged to check the annual tax assessment and correct any errors or omissions. The deadline for corrections is normally 1 May.
- Tax refunds are typically paid out in April. Back tax can be paid voluntarily; otherwise it is collected with an interest surcharge, either in instalments or via the following year's preliminary income assessment.
- In the first year, newcomers often receive an annual tax assessment that needs to be checked especially carefully, because the Danish Tax Agency does not know about income before the move or about foreign circumstances.

8.9 Tax return (oplysningsskema) and foreign income:
- People with foreign income, foreign bank accounts, foreign property, foreign shares or a self-employed business receive a tax return (oplysningsskema) instead of a finished annual tax assessment. The deadline is normally 1 July.
- If you are fully liable to tax, you must declare foreign interest, dividends, rental income, pensions and salary. Double taxation is avoided through relief (credit or exemption) under the double taxation agreement.
- Foreign bank accounts and securities accounts must be reported to the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen). Countries automatically exchange account information (CRS).
- If you own real property abroad, you must declare it, and you may have to pay Danish property value tax (ejendomsværdiskat).

8.10 Shares, investments and cryptocurrency:
- Share income (gains and dividends) is taxed at 27 % up to an annual threshold (approx. DKK 67,500 in 2025; double for married couples) and at 42 % on amounts above that.
- When you move to Denmark, shares and other securities are given a tax entry value equal to their market value at the time of moving, so that only increases in value after the move are taxed in Denmark.
- Share savings account (aktiesparekonto): A special account with low tax (17 %) and a ceiling on deposits.
- Investment funds and ETFs may be taxed on a mark-to-market basis (lagerbeskatning - tax on the year's increase in value, even without a sale).
- Gains on cryptocurrency are taxable and must be declared by yourself.
- When moving away from Denmark, exit tax (fraflytterskat) may be calculated on larger shareholdings and on pension schemes.

8.11 Special tax schemes (the researcher tax scheme, forskerskatteordningen / the 27 % scheme):
- Highly paid employees and researchers recruited from abroad who have not been liable to tax in Denmark in the past 10 years can, under strict conditions (including a set minimum monthly salary that is adjusted annually), apply to be taxed under the researcher tax scheme (gross tax of 27 % + 8 % labour market contribution for up to 7 years). The application must be submitted by the employer.
- The total tax under the scheme is 32.84 % of salary, and you cannot use deductions against that income.
- The minimum salary was approx. DKK 78,000 a month in 2025 (after ATP). It has been politically decided to lower the salary requirement significantly from 2026 (to around DKK 63,000 a month). Check the current amount on skat.dk.
- Approved researchers are exempt from the salary requirement.
- The scheme must be chosen at the start of employment, and the employer must be Danish or have a permanent establishment in Denmark.
- After the 7 years (84 months), you switch to ordinary taxation.

8.12 Cross-border commuters (grænsegængere) and commuters:
- People who live abroad and work in Denmark have limited tax liability and pay Danish tax on their Danish salary.
- The cross-border commuter rule (grænsegængerreglen): If you earn at least 75 % of your total income in Denmark, you can choose to receive the same deductions as people with full tax liability (e.g. interest expenses and transport).
- Between Denmark and Sweden, the Øresund Agreement (Øresundsaftalen) applies, and between Denmark and Germany, a double taxation agreement with special rules for working from home.
- Labour hire (arbejdsudleje): Foreign employees who are hired out to a Danish company are taxed under special rules with a gross tax.

8.13 Other taxes and duties that newcomers encounter:
- VAT (moms): 25 % on almost all goods and services, including food. Prices in shops always include VAT.
- Registration tax (registreringsafgift) on cars (see the section on cars).
- Property tax (land tax, grundskyld) and property value tax (ejendomsværdiskat) for homeowners.
- Duties on electricity, petrol, tobacco, alcohol, sugary products and packaging (deposit return system, pant).
- The media licence has been abolished; DR (the Danish public broadcaster) is financed through taxes.
- Gifts and inheritance: Gifts above an annual threshold between close family members are subject to tax. Gifts from abroad to people resident in Denmark may also be covered.

8.14 Contact and help with tax:
- The Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) can be contacted by telephone on 72 22 18 18 and via skat.dk. English-language guidance is available at skat.dk/english.
- The Danish Tax Agency never sends text messages or emails with links asking you to enter card details to get money back. Tax refunds are always paid automatically into your NemKonto.

[SECTION 9: BANK ACCOUNT, NEMKONTO AND PAYMENTS]
Keywords: bank, bankkonto, lønkonto, NemKonto, basal betalingskonto, hvidvask, KYC, netbank, MobilePay, Dankort, betalingskort, Betalingsservice, IBAN, kredit, lån, RKI, bank account, open account, salary account, basic payment account, anti-money laundering, online banking, debit card, direct debit, credit check, loan, payment card, money transfer abroad, credit assessment

9.1 Opening a Danish bank account:
- To receive your salary from a Danish employer and pay bills (Betalingsservice/MobilePay), a Danish bank account is necessary in practice.
- Anti-money laundering rules (KYC - Know Your Customer): Danish banks are subject to strict legal requirements for identification and documentation.
- What does the bank typically require?
  * CPR number (personal identification number).
  * Valid passport (some banks require a passport rather than a national ID card).
  * Danish address registered in the Civil Registration System (folkeregisteradresse).
  * Employment contract or proof of enrolment (documentation of the purpose of the customer relationship and the origin of the funds).
  * Tax identification number (TIN - Tax Identification Number) from your home country.
  * MitID (makes the process significantly faster).
- Processing time: It can take anything from a few days to several weeks to have an account opened at a Danish bank. It is recommended to contact the bank in good time.
- Right to a basic payment account: Under Danish law, everyone legally resident in the EU has the right to a basic payment account (basal betalingskonto) at a Danish bank, unless specific anti-money laundering reasons weigh against it.

9.2 Basic payment account (basal betalingskonto) and refusal by the bank:
- A basic payment account is an account with the basic functions: depositing and withdrawing money, receiving salary, paying bills and having a payment card. There is a ceiling on what the bank may charge in fees.
- The bank must process the request within approx. 10 banking days and may only refuse for specific reasons, e.g. if the anti-money laundering rules cannot be complied with.
- A refusal must be in writing and state the reasons. You can complain to the bank's complaints officer and then to Det finansielle ankenævn (the Danish Financial Complaint Board).
- A basic payment account does not give the right to credit, loans or an overdraft facility.

9.3 Choosing a bank:
- The largest banks in Denmark include Danske Bank, Nordea, Jyske Bank, Nykredit Bank, Sydbank and Arbejdernes Landsbank. There are also many local savings banks and purely digital banks.
- Several banks have special departments or programmes for international customers and can serve customers in English.
- Compare fees for accounts, cards and international transfers. Many banks charge a monthly customer fee.
- The interest rate on ordinary salary accounts is low. Savings earn better interest in special savings accounts.

9.4 Payment cards and everyday payments:
- Dankort is the national debit card and is most often issued as a combined Visa/Dankort. Many banks also issue Mastercard Debit.
- Denmark is almost cashless. Cards, mobile payments (Apple Pay/Google Pay) and MobilePay are used everywhere, and some shops do not accept cash in the evening.
- New customers often first receive a debit card with balance control. Credit cards require a credit assessment.
- The card and PIN are sent in separate letters to your address registered in the Civil Registration System (folkeregisteradresse). Your name must be on the letterbox.

9.5 MobilePay:
- MobilePay is a very widely used app for transferring money between private individuals and for paying in shops and online shops.
- The requirements are normally a Danish CPR number, a Danish mobile number, an account with a Danish bank and MitID. Users must be at least 13 years old.
- Without MobilePay, it can be difficult to split bills, buy second-hand items and pay small amounts in clubs and associations and at flea markets.

9.6 Betalingsservice and bills:
- Betalingsservice (BS) is a direct debit scheme in which regular bills (rent, electricity, insurance, telephone) are automatically deducted from your account. You sign up each bill in online banking or using the details on the bill.
- Payment slips (indbetalingskort) have a code beginning with +71, +73 or +75 and are paid in online banking.
- If a bill is paid late, reminder fees (typically DKK 100 per reminder) and interest are added. Unpaid bills can end up with debt collection and lead to registration as a bad payer.

9.7 NemKonto:
- A NemKonto is a completely ordinary bank account that you designate as your primary account for payments from the public sector.
- All payments from public authorities - tax refunds, SU (the Danish state educational grant), child benefit (børne- og ungeydelse), housing benefit (boligstøtte), unemployment benefits (dagpenge) and pension - are automatically paid into your NemKonto.
- Setting up a NemKonto: When you open a Danish bank account, the bank can register the account as your NemKonto straight away. You can also register or change your NemKonto yourself on nemkonto.dk with MitID.
- Foreign account as NemKonto: It is also possible to register a foreign bank account (with IBAN and BIC/SWIFT) as your NemKonto via nemkonto.dk (this may require manual approval with documentation of account ownership).
- You can only have one NemKonto at a time. Employers can also pay your salary into your NemKonto if you have not provided another account number.
- If you have no NemKonto, payments from the public sector may be delayed or sent as a payment order (anvisning).

9.8 Transfers to and from abroad:
- Transfers in euros within the EU/EEA (SEPA) are cheap and fast. Transfers in other currencies and to countries outside the EU involve a fee and an exchange rate margin.
- The Danish krone (DKK) has a fixed exchange rate against the euro (approx. DKK 7.46 per euro).
- Banks may ask about the purpose of larger transfers and request documentation because of the anti-money laundering rules.
- When entering or leaving the EU, cash of EUR 10,000 or more must be declared to customs.

9.9 Loans, credit and credit assessment:
- Denmark does not have a central system with a personal credit score as in, for example, the USA. Instead, banks assess your income, debt and disposable income based on payslips, the annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse) and bank statements.
- RKI and Debitor Registret are private registers of bad payers. If you are registered, it is difficult to get a loan, a mobile subscription or rented housing.
- Newcomers have no Danish credit history and may therefore be asked to pay a deposit, e.g. for a mobile subscription.
- Home loans: Buying a home is typically financed with a mortgage loan from a mortgage credit institution (realkreditlån, up to 80 % of the price), a bank loan (up to 15 %) and your own down payment (at least 5 %).
- Quick loans and consumer loans have high costs and should be avoided.

9.10 Banking fraud and security:
- Your bank never asks for MitID codes or PIN codes, or asks you to move money to a "safe account".
- Lending your bank account to others (acting as a money mule) is a criminal offence, even if you have merely "helped an acquaintance".
- A lost card can be blocked around the clock via Nets/the bank's card blocking service or in the bank's app.

[SECTION 10: HOUSING, TENANCY AGREEMENT AND BUYING A HOME]
Keywords: bolig, lejebolig, lejlighed, værelse, lejekontrakt, typeformular, depositum, forudbetalt leje, indflytningssyn, fraflytning, opsigelse, huslejenævn, fremleje, almen bolig, boligforening, andelsbolig, ejerbolig, boligstøtte, boligsikring, kollegium, boligsvindel, housing, rent, apartment, room, lease, tenancy agreement, deposit, prepaid rent, move-in inspection, notice period, rent tribunal, sublet, social housing, housing association, cooperative housing, buy property, housing benefit, dormitory, rental scam, landlord, flat, move-out inspection, house rules

10.1 The housing market for newcomers:
- It is difficult and expensive to find housing in Copenhagen and Aarhus, especially in August and September. Start your housing search before you arrive.
- Private rental housing can be found via housing portals such as BoligPortal, Lejebolig.dk, Findbolig.nu and DBA, as well as via Facebook groups and personal networks. Some portals require a paid subscription to contact landlords.
- Public (non-profit) housing (almene boliger, also called social housing) is let by housing organisations according to a waiting list. There is a small annual fee to be on the list, and the waiting time in the large cities is often many years. Anyone can sign up.
- In special social cases, the municipality (kommune) can allocate a home, but it normally has no obligation to find housing for newcomers.
- Many employers and relocation agencies help international employees find housing.

10.2 Types of housing:
- Rental housing (lejebolig): Private rental or public (non-profit) housing (almen bolig).
- Room (værelse): A room in someone else's home, often with access to a kitchen and bathroom. The tenant has weaker protection, and the notice period is shorter.
- Halls of residence (kollegium) and youth housing (ungdomsbolig): For students and young people in education. Applications are made via central waiting lists in the university cities.
- Cooperative housing (andelsbolig): You buy a share in an association that owns the property and get the right to use a flat. The price is lower than for an owner-occupied home, but it normally cannot be financed with a mortgage loan (realkreditlån).
- Owner-occupied home (ejerbolig): An owner-occupied flat or a house that you own outright.

10.3 Tenancy agreement (lejekontrakt) and deposit (depositum):
- When renting a home in Denmark, the standard form "Typeformular A, 10. udgave" (Standard Form A, 10th edition) is almost always used.
- It is legal and normal for the landlord to require up to 3 months' rent as a deposit plus up to 3 months' prepaid rent (forudbetalt leje) when you move in.
- Together with the first month's rent, moving in can therefore cost up to 7 months' rent.
- § 11 of the tenancy agreement contains special terms that deviate from the Rent Act (lejeloven). Read them carefully, e.g. regarding maintenance, pets, smoking and redecoration when moving out.
- The tenancy agreement should always be in writing and signed by both parties. It is often signed digitally with MitID.
- The deposit and prepaid rent should be paid by bank transfer so that there is documentation. Never pay in cash without a receipt.

10.4 Moving in and the move-in inspection (indflytningssyn):
- Landlords who let more than one home are obliged to hold a move-in inspection together with the tenant and prepare a move-in report (indflytningsrapport).
- The tenant has 14 days from the start of the tenancy to report faults and defects to the landlord in writing, so that the tenant is not held liable for them when moving out.
- Take dated photos of the entire home when you move in, and keep them together with the report.
- Read the electricity, water and heating meters on the day you move in.

10.5 Rent, utilities and rent increases:
- Rent (husleje) is paid in advance, normally on the 1st of the month.
- In addition to the rent, you typically make on-account payments (a conto) for heating and water. Once a year a utility statement (forbrugsregnskab) is prepared, and you either get money back or have to pay extra.
- The rent can be adjusted according to the rules in the Rent Act (lejeloven), e.g. in line with the net price index, if this has been agreed in the contract. The landlord cannot raise the rent freely.
- If you believe the rent is too high, you can have it assessed by the Rent Tribunal (Huslejenævnet).

10.6 Termination (opsigelse) and fixed-term tenancies:
- The tenant can normally terminate the lease of a flat with 3 months' notice to the 1st of a month. For single rooms, the notice period is typically 1 month.
- The landlord can only terminate the tenancy in special cases, e.g. if the landlord is going to live in the home themselves (with 1 year's notice), or if the tenant is in breach of the agreement.
- Fixed-term tenancy agreement (tidsbegrænset lejekontrakt): The tenancy ends automatically on the end date. The time limit must be justified by the landlord's circumstances. During the period, the tenant can normally give notice unless otherwise agreed.
- If the rent is not paid on time, the landlord can send a payment demand (påkrav) with a fee and, after a deadline of 14 days, terminate the tenancy.

10.7 Moving out and repayment of the deposit (depositum):
- The home must be handed back in the same condition as when you moved in, apart from ordinary wear and tear. The landlord may only require redecoration that is necessary and cannot require the home to be newly redecorated (nyistandsættelse) if it was not newly redecorated when you moved in.
- Landlords with more than one home must hold a move-out inspection (fraflytningssyn) and hand over a move-out report. Claims for redecoration must be made within short deadlines.
- The deposit is used to cover redecoration and any unpaid rent. The rest must be repaid within a reasonable time, typically 1-2 months, together with a statement of account.
- Disagreement about the deposit is the most common conflict between tenants and landlords and can be brought before the Rent Tribunal (Huslejenævnet).

10.8 Help with disputes about rental housing:
- The Rent Tribunal (Huslejenævnet) in the municipality decides disputes about rent, deposits, maintenance and utility statements in private rental housing. There is a fee of a few hundred kroner to bring a case.
- The Residents' Complaints Board (Beboerklagenævnet) handles similar cases in public (non-profit) housing (almene boliger).
- Lejernes Landsorganisation (LLO, the National Tenants' Association) and other tenants' associations advise and help their members.
- The rules are set out in the Rent Act (lejeloven), which protects the tenant to a large extent and cannot be deviated from to the tenant's detriment.

10.9 Subletting (fremleje) and shared housing:
- A tenant may sublet no more than half of the home's rooms without the landlord's permission, provided the number of occupants does not exceed the number of habitable rooms.
- The entire flat may be sublet for up to 2 years in case of temporary absence (e.g. study abroad, posting abroad or illness), unless the landlord has a reasonable ground to refuse.
- The sublease agreement must be in writing, and the landlord must have a copy. The subtenant can normally have their address registered (bopælsregistrering) at the property.
- Illegal subletting can mean that both parties lose the home.

10.10 Rental scams (boligsvindel) - warning signs:
- The landlord is "abroad" and cannot show you the home.
- You are asked to pay a deposit before you have seen the home and signed a contract.
- Payment must be made via money transfer services, cryptocurrency or to a foreign account.
- The price is suspiciously low for the area, or the landlord pushes for a quick decision.
- The landlord will not let you register your address (bopælsregistrering) at the property. This suggests illegal subletting and means that you cannot get a CPR number.
- Check who owns the home on tinglysning.dk (the Danish land registry) or in BBR/OIS, and ask to see the landlord's ID.

10.11 Housing benefit (boligstøtte):
- If you rent a year-round home with its own kitchen, you can apply for housing benefit (boligstøtte, financial support towards the rent) via borger.dk. The amount depends on the rent, the size of the home, the number of occupants and the household's total income and assets.
- Housing benefit is administered by Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark) and is called boligsikring (rent allowance) for most people and boligydelse (housing allowance) for pensioners.
- You normally cannot get housing benefit for a room without its own kitchen. The application should be submitted within 30 days of moving in to receive support from the moving-in date.
- Everyone in the household must have their address registered at the property, and changes in income or household must be reported to Udbetaling Danmark.
- The municipality can give loans for the resident's deposit (beboerindskud) in public (non-profit) housing (almene boliger) if your income is low.

10.12 Buying a home as a foreigner:
- People who are not resident in Denmark and have not previously lived here for a total of 5 years must, as a rule, have permission from the Civil Affairs Agency (Civilstyrelsen) to buy real property.
- EU/EEA citizens can buy a year-round home without permission if the home is to be used as their permanent residence or for business purposes, provided they submit a declaration to that effect.
- Third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) with a residence and work permit are normally granted permission to buy a year-round home that they will live in themselves, possibly with a condition that it must be sold if they leave the country.
- Summer houses and holiday homes are subject to strict rules, and foreigners are only granted permission if they have a particularly strong connection to Denmark.
- Buying a home typically involves an estate agent (ejendomsmægler, who represents the seller), your own buyer's adviser or lawyer, a bank and a mortgage credit institution (realkreditinstitut). The purchase is registered through digital land registration (tinglysning) for a registration fee (tinglysningsafgift).
- Homeowners pay property tax (ejendomsskat, i.e. land tax or grundskyld) and property value tax (ejendomsværdiskat), as well as any common charges to the owners' association (ejerforening).

10.13 House rules (husorden) and neighbours:
- Most buildings have house rules covering noise (typically quiet after 22:00 on weekdays), laundry times, waste, pets and use of common areas.
- Pets are often prohibited or require permission in rental housing.
- In smaller buildings it is normal to take care of cleaning the stairwell or clearing snow yourself if this is stated in the contract.

[SECTION 11: ELECTRICITY, WATER, HEATING, INTERNET, MOBILE, WASTE, POST AND INSURANCE]
Keywords: el, strøm, elleverandør, elpris, vand, varme, fjernvarme, internet, bredbånd, fiber, mobilabonnement, taletidskort, SIM-kort, affald, affaldssortering, genbrug, pant, postkasse, post, pakke, forsikring, indboforsikring, ansvarsforsikring, ulykkesforsikring, electricity, power supplier, utilities, water, heating, district heating, broadband, mobile subscription, prepaid SIM, waste sorting, recycling, bottle deposit, mailbox, parcel, insurance, home contents insurance, liability insurance, electricity bill, power socket, tap water, mobile phone, travel insurance, car insurance

11.1 Electricity:
- You must sign up with an electricity supplier (elleverandør) yourself when you move in; otherwise you risk having your power cut off. Prices and products can be compared on elpris.dk.
- The electricity bill consists of the electricity price itself (paid to the electricity supplier), transport (paid to the grid company) and duties and VAT (paid to the state). Duties and transport make up a large part of the bill.
- Most people have hourly billing, where the electricity price varies from hour to hour. Electricity is typically most expensive in the morning and between 17:00 and 21:00, and cheapest at night.
- Signing up normally requires a CPR number. When you move, you must notify your electricity supplier of the move.
- Power sockets in Denmark have a special Danish earth connection (type K), but standard European plugs (types C and F) fit the sockets. The mains voltage is 230 volts.

11.2 Water and heating:
- Water and heating are often paid on account (a conto) to the landlord via the rent, but in some homes you must sign up with the utility company yourself.
- District heating (fjernvarme) is the most common form of heating in the cities. Houses are also heated by heat pumps, natural gas and wood pellets.
- Tap water can be drunk everywhere in Denmark and is of high quality. Water is relatively expensive because the price includes wastewater drainage and duties.
- Air out your home several times a day for a short time to avoid damp and mould. Tenants can be held liable for damage caused by insufficient airing.

11.3 Internet and TV:
- Internet is delivered via fibre, cable TV networks, copper (DSL) or mobile broadband (4G/5G). What is available depends on the address.
- In many flats there is a joint agreement through the building or the antenna association (antenneforening), which may be compulsory or give a discount.
- Subscriptions typically have a commitment period of no more than 6 months for private customers.
- There is no TV licence. DR's public channels can be watched for free via DRTV.

11.4 Mobile phone:
- Prepaid SIM cards (taletidskort) can be bought in kiosks and supermarkets without a CPR number and are a good solution for the first few weeks.
- A mobile subscription normally requires a CPR number and often a credit assessment (kreditvurdering) or payment by payment card.
- A Danish mobile number (+45, 8 digits) is in practice necessary for MobilePay, your bank, your doctor and contact with the authorities.
- The EU roaming rules mean that subscriptions from other EU countries can be used in Denmark at home-country prices for a period, but not permanently.

11.5 Waste, recycling and the deposit return system (pant):
- Households must sort their waste into a number of fractions: food waste, paper, cardboard, glass, metal, plastic, food and drink cartons, textiles, hazardous waste and residual waste. The schemes and bins vary from municipality to municipality.
- Bulky waste, electronics, furniture and garden waste are taken to the recycling centre (genbrugsplads) or collected under the municipality's scheme.
- Deposit return system (pant): There is a deposit on most bottles and cans of beer, soft drinks, water and juice. Deposit label A gives DKK 1, B gives DKK 1.50 and C gives DKK 3. The packaging is returned to deposit return machines (pantautomater) in supermarkets.
- Batteries and electronics must not be put in the residual waste.

11.6 Mailbox, letters and parcels:
- The resident's full name must be clearly displayed on the mailbox at the home. Without a name on the mailbox, letters will not be delivered, including health cards (sundhedskort), bank cards and PIN codes.
- All adults in the household must be listed on the mailbox, including subtenants and lodgers.
- PostNord has stopped delivering letters in Denmark, and letter delivery is now handled by other distributors such as DAO. Letters do not necessarily arrive every day.
- Parcels are delivered by PostNord, GLS, DAO, Bring and DHL, among others, most often to parcel shops and parcel lockers, where they are collected with a code.
- You can put a "Reklamer - Nej tak" ("No advertising, thank you") sticker on your mailbox to avoid unaddressed advertising.
- For goods ordered from countries outside the EU, you must pay VAT (moms, 25 %) and possibly customs duty, as well as a handling fee.

11.7 Insurance that most people in Denmark have:
- Contents insurance (indboforsikring, also called familieforsikring): Covers your belongings in case of fire, theft and water damage. It almost always includes liability insurance and legal expenses insurance. The landlord's insurance only covers the building, not the tenant's belongings.
- Liability insurance (ansvarsforsikring): Covers you if you happen to injure other people or damage their property. It is not compulsory for private individuals but is considered necessary.
- Accident insurance (ulykkesforsikring): Pays compensation for permanent injury after accidents. The employer's insurance only covers you during working hours.
- Travel insurance (rejseforsikring): Necessary when travelling, as the yellow health card (sundhedskort) does not cover you abroad.
- Car insurance (bilforsikring): Liability insurance is compulsory for all motor vehicles. Comprehensive insurance (kasko) is voluntary.
- Dog insurance (hundeforsikring): Liability insurance is compulsory for all dogs.
- Home insurance (husforsikring): For homeowners.
- Life insurance and insurance against critical illness and loss of earning capacity are often part of the occupational pension (arbejdsmarkedspension).
- Insurance companies ask for your CPR number and previous claims history. Documentation of claim-free years from abroad can give a discount on car insurance.

[SECTION 12: LABOUR MARKET, EMPLOYMENT, PAY, HOLIDAY AND PARENTAL LEAVE]
Keywords: arbejde, job, jobsøgning, ansættelseskontrakt, ansættelsesbevis, løn, mindsteløn, overenskomst, fagforening, funktionær, opsigelse, prøvetid, arbejdstid, overarbejde, ferie, feriepenge, FerieKonto, sygdom, sygedagpenge, barsel, barselsdagpenge, arbejdsmiljø, Arbejdstilsynet, helligdage, work, employment, job search, employment contract, salary, minimum wage, collective agreement, trade union, notice period, probation, working hours, overtime, holiday, holiday pay, sick leave, parental leave, maternity leave, paternity leave, working environment, public holidays, wage, Salaried Employees Act, Holiday Act, sickness benefit, work culture

12.1 Job searching in Denmark:
- Jobs can be found on Jobnet.dk (the public job site), Jobindex.dk, LinkedIn and companies' own websites, and via temping agencies, among others.
- Workindenmark.dk is the public English-language job portal for international applicants and offers free help with job searching.
- EURES is the EU's job portal for jobs across national borders.
- Many jobs are filled through networks and unsolicited applications. It is common to contact a company directly.
- A Danish CV is short (1-2 pages), in reverse chronological order and accompanied by a targeted application letter of no more than one page. A photo is optional. The employer may ask for references and, for some jobs, a criminal record certificate (straffeattest) or child protection certificate (børneattest).

12.2 Employment contract (ansættelseskontrakt):
- If your working hours exceed 3 hours a week on average over 4 weeks, you are entitled to a written statement of employment (ansættelsesbevis)/employment contract no later than 7 days (for essential terms) or 1 month after the start of employment.
- Among other things, the contract must state the place of work, job title, start date, pay and supplements, working hours, holiday, notice periods, probationary period and which collective agreement (overenskomst) applies.
- Read the contract carefully before you sign it. A trade union (fagforening) can review it for its members.
- Special clauses such as non-compete and non-solicitation clauses (konkurrence- og kundeklausuler) restrict what you may do after the job ends and must meet legal requirements, including on compensation.

12.3 The Danish model, collective agreements and pay:
- The Danish labour market is based on "the Danish model" (den danske model), in which pay and working conditions are largely agreed between trade unions and employers' organisations (collective agreements, overenskomster). There is no statutory minimum wage in Denmark.
- A collective agreement typically sets the minimum wage, supplements for overtime and unsocial working hours, pension, pay during sickness and parental leave, extra days of holiday and notice periods.
- If the workplace is not covered by a collective agreement, only the legislation and the terms of the individual contract apply. It is therefore important to negotiate your pay and pension yourself.
- The minimum wage in most collective agreements is in the range of DKK 130-150 per hour for unskilled adults, but it varies between sectors.
- Pay is normally paid monthly in arrears, at the end of the month, into your bank account. Pay is typically negotiated once a year.
- Undeclared work (sort arbejde, work without paying tax) is illegal and gives no right to holiday pay, pension, industrial injury insurance or unemployment benefits (dagpenge).

12.4 The Salaried Employees Act (Funktionærloven) and dismissal:
- Salaried employees (funktionærer) are employees with office, commercial, warehouse, technical or managerial duties who work more than 8 hours a week. They are covered by the Salaried Employees Act.
- Probationary period (prøvetid): A probationary period of up to 3 months can be agreed, during which the employer can dismiss you with 14 days' notice.
- The employer's notice period (opsigelsesvarsel) under the Salaried Employees Act increases with length of service: 1 month during the first approx. 6 months, then 3 months, rising gradually to 6 months after approx. 9 years of employment.
- The employee's own notice period is 1 month to the end of a month.
- For employees who are not salaried employees, the notice periods are set out in the collective agreement or contract and are often shorter.
- A dismissal must be objectively justified. Unfair dismissal can give a right to compensation. It is illegal to dismiss someone because of pregnancy, parental leave, gender, age, disability, religion, ethnicity, sexual orientation or trade union membership.

12.5 Working hours:
- A normal full-time week is 37 hours, typically spread over Monday to Friday. The lunch break is often 30 minutes and may be paid or unpaid.
- The average weekly working time must not exceed 48 hours including overtime, measured over 4 months. You are entitled to 11 hours' rest in every 24-hour period and one day off a week.
- Employers must have a system for recording employees' daily working hours.
- Flexible working hours and working from home are widespread, and there is great respect for employees leaving early to pick up their children.
- Overtime is paid according to the collective agreement or contract. For many salaried employees, overtime is included in their salary.

12.6 Trade unions (fagforening):
- Trade union (fagforening): Protects your rights in the workplace, provides legal assistance and checks employment contracts. Membership is voluntary and tax-deductible (up to an annual ceiling).
- Most trade unions are organised by trade or education (e.g. 3F, HK, IDA, Djøf, Dansk Sygeplejeråd, Dansk Magisterforening). There are also cross-sector ("yellow") trade unions, which are cheaper but normally do not negotiate collective agreements.
- The trade union can take legal action on behalf of a member in cases of unpaid wages, unfair dismissal, industrial injury and bankruptcy.
- Many trade unions offer guidance in English and special offers for international members.
- The union representative (tillidsrepræsentant) is the colleagues' elected representative in the workplace and the first point of contact if problems arise.

12.7 The Holiday Act (Ferieloven) and holiday pay (feriepenge):
- All employees in Denmark accrue 2.08 days of holiday with pay or holiday allowance (feriegodtgørelse, 12.5 % of pay) per month, which corresponds to 5 weeks' (25 days') paid holiday a year.
- Denmark has concurrent holiday (samtidighedsferie): Accrued holiday can be taken as early as the month after it has been accrued. The accrual year runs from 1 September to 31 August, and the holiday can be taken in the holiday period from 1 September to 31 December of the following year (16 months).
- Employees paid monthly normally receive their salary during holiday plus a holiday supplement (ferietillæg) of at least 1 %. Employees paid by the hour instead have 12.5 % of their pay paid into FerieKonto or a holiday fund (feriekasse) and receive the money when they take holiday.
- Holiday pay is paid out when you request it on borger.dk ("Feriepenge") with MitID.
- You are entitled to take 3 consecutive weeks of holiday (the main holiday, hovedferie) in the period 1 May to 30 September.
- Many collective agreements and contracts give a 6th week of holiday (5 extra days off, feriefridage).
- When you leave a job, the employer pays the accrued holiday pay into FerieKonto. If you leave Denmark permanently, you can have your holiday pay paid out.
- New employees who have not yet accrued holiday can agree to take holiday in advance or take unpaid holiday.

12.8 Public holidays (helligdage):
- Official public holidays: New Year's Day, Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, Easter Sunday, Easter Monday, Ascension Day, Whit Sunday, Whit Monday, Christmas Day and Boxing Day.
- Great Prayer Day (Store bededag) has been abolished as a public holiday.
- Constitution Day (Grundlovsdag, 5 June), 1 May, Christmas Eve (24 December) and New Year's Eve (31 December) are not public holidays, but many people have a full or partial day off under their collective agreement or contract.
- Most shops are open on ordinary Sundays, but larger shops are closed on public holidays.

12.9 Sickness:
- You must report sick to your employer as soon as possible, normally at the start of working hours on the first day of illness.
- Salaried employees and many employees covered by collective agreements receive full pay during sickness. Others receive sickness benefit (sygedagpenge), which the employer pays for the first 30 days if you have been employed for at least 8 weeks and have worked at least 74 hours.
- In case of longer illness, the employer can ask for a doctor's note (friattest) or a fitness-for-work statement (mulighedserklæring) from your doctor, but is not entitled to know the diagnosis.
- In case of longer illness, the municipality (kommune) will call you in for follow-up.
- Child's first day of illness: Many collective agreements give the right to time off with pay on the child's first (and often second) day of illness.

12.10 Maternity/paternity leave (barsel) and parental leave (forældreorlov):
- The mother is entitled to 4 weeks of pregnancy leave before the expected birth and 10 weeks of leave after the birth (the first 2 weeks are compulsory).
- The father or co-mother is entitled to 2 weeks of leave in connection with the birth.
- In addition, each parent is entitled to parental leave. In total, each parent as a rule has 24 weeks of leave with parental leave benefit (barselsdagpenge) after the birth.
- For employees, 11 weeks are earmarked for each parent and cannot be transferred. The remaining weeks can be transferred to the other parent.
- Parental leave benefit is paid by Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark) and is at most just under DKK 5,000 per week before tax (2025-26 level). Entitlement requires that you meet an employment requirement.
- Many employers pay full or partial salary for part of the leave under the collective agreement or contract.
- The employer must be notified of the pregnancy no later than 3 months before the expected birth. Pregnant women and parents on leave are specially protected against dismissal.
- Single parents and LGBT+ families have special options for transferring leave.
- Under EU rules, EU citizens can have insurance and employment periods from other EU countries counted towards the employment requirement.

12.11 Working environment and industrial injuries (arbejdsskade):
- The employer is responsible for ensuring that work is safe and healthy and must instruct employees in a language they understand.
- The Danish Working Environment Authority (Arbejdstilsynet) carries out inspections and can be contacted anonymously about dangerous work. Arbejdstilsynet has a hotline for foreign workers.
- All employers must have industrial injury insurance (arbejdsskadeforsikring). Accidents at work and occupational diseases are reported to the insurer and to Labour Market Insurance (Arbejdsmarkedets Erhvervssikring, AES).
- It is the employer's responsibility to prevent and deal with bullying, harassment and sexual harassment.

12.12 Working culture in Danish workplaces:
- The hierarchy is flat. You are on first-name terms with your boss, and you are expected to speak your mind and take independent responsibility.
- Meetings start on time, and punctuality is important.
- Work-life balance is highly valued. Most people go home between 16:00 and 17:00.
- Social traditions at the workplace include shared lunch, breakfast bread (morgenbrød), Friday bar (fredagsbar), a summer party and a Christmas lunch (julefrokost).
- Communication is direct and informal, which may seem blunt to newcomers but is rarely meant to be impolite.

12.13 Posted workers and foreign companies:
- Foreign companies that temporarily provide services in Denmark must register in the Register of Foreign Service Providers (Registret for Udenlandske Tjenesteydere, RUT).
- Posted workers (udstationerede) are covered by Danish rules on matters such as working environment, equal treatment and holiday during their stay.
- Social security: An A1 certificate documents which country's social security system you are covered by when working across borders in the EU.

[SECTION 13: A-KASSE (UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE FUND), UNEMPLOYMENT BENEFITS, SOCIAL BENEFITS AND PENSION]
Keywords: a-kasse, arbejdsløshedskasse, dagpenge, ledig, arbejdsløs, jobcenter, Jobnet, PD U1, kontanthjælp, sociale ydelser, Udbetaling Danmark, pension, folkepension, ATP, arbejdsmarkedspension, pensionsalder, unemployment insurance, unemployment benefits, unemployed, job centre, social assistance, welfare benefits, state pension, occupational pension, retirement age, unemployment insurance fund, Payout Denmark, pension when leaving Denmark

13.1 A-kasse (unemployment insurance fund):
- A-kasse: Insurance against unemployment (unemployment benefits, dagpenge). Membership is voluntary and tax-deductible.
- The a-kasse is not the same as the trade union (fagforening), although they often work together and can be paid for together.
- The membership fee is typically approx. DKK 500-600 per month before tax deduction.
- You can be a member as an employee, a self-employed person or a student. Students on a programme lasting at least 18 months can often be members free of charge and become entitled to unemployment benefits as recent graduates.

13.2 Conditions for unemployment benefits (dagpenge):
- To become entitled to unemployment benefits, you normally need 1 year of membership of an a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) and must meet an income requirement (a total income of approx. DKK 263,000 within the last 3 years at 2025 level, with a ceiling on how much can be counted per month).
- You must be registered as unemployed on Jobnet.dk from your first day of unemployment, live and stay in Denmark, be available for the labour market and actively look for work.
- Unemployment benefits amount to at most 90 % of your previous pay, up to a maximum (approx. DKK 21,000-22,000 per month before tax for those insured full-time).
- The benefit period is up to 2 years within 3 years.
- If you resign without a valid reason, you are given a quarantine period (karantæne) of a few weeks.
- Third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) with a residence permit tied to a specific job may find it difficult to meet the requirement to be available for the labour market. Ask the a-kasse before you join.

13.3 Transfer of insurance periods from other EU/EEA countries (PD U1):
- If you have been insured against unemployment in another EU/EEA country, insurance periods can often be transferred via the document PD U1 – this requires prompt membership of a Danish a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) within 8 weeks after your insurance abroad ends.
- Normally you must also have worked in Denmark for a period after joining before the foreign periods can be counted.
- The PD U1 is issued by the authority in the country where you were last insured.
- PD U2: An unemployed person receiving unemployment benefits (dagpenge) can take their Danish unemployment benefits to another EU country for up to 3 months to look for work.

13.4 Job centre (jobcenter) and the municipality's employment services:
- Unemployed people are called in for meetings at the a-kasse and the municipality's job centre and must have an updated CV on Jobnet.
- The employment system is being restructured so that municipalities get greater freedom to organise their efforts.
- The municipality can offer work placements (virksomhedspraktik), wage-subsidised jobs (løntilskudsjob) and upskilling.

13.5 Social assistance (kontanthjælp) and residence requirements:
- Social assistance is the municipality's lowest subsistence benefit for people who cannot support themselves and are not entitled to other benefits. It depends on your assets and your spouse's income.
- To receive the standard rate, you must meet a residence requirement (legal residence in Denmark for at least 9 of the last 10 years) and an employment requirement (full-time work for approx. 2.5 years within the last 10 years). If you do not meet the requirements, you receive a lower rate.
- Third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) with a residence permit based on work, studies or family reunification must be able to support themselves. Receiving social assistance can lead to the residence permit being revoked, and it delays the possibility of permanent residence and citizenship.
- EU citizens with worker status are entitled to equal treatment, but an EU citizen looking for work without worker status is normally not entitled to social assistance.

13.6 Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark):
- Udbetaling Danmark is the authority that pays out most nationwide benefits: state pension (folkepension), disability pension (førtidspension), housing benefit (boligstøtte), parental leave benefit (barselsdagpenge), child benefit (børne- og ungeydelse), child allowance (børnetilskud), child maintenance (børnebidrag) and international social security.
- Self-service is on borger.dk with MitID. Decisions are sent via Digital Post.
- You are obliged to report changes, e.g. moving, changed income, cohabitation or stays abroad. Any benefit paid in excess must be repaid.

13.7 The pension system in Denmark:
- State pension (folkepension): The public basic pension. The state pension age is 67 and is rising gradually (68 in 2030, 69 in 2035 and 70 in 2040) in line with life expectancy.
- Entitlement to state pension is earned through residence in Denmark. A full state pension requires approx. 40 years of residence between the age of 15 and pension age (for younger age groups, 9/10 of the period). If you have lived here for a shorter time, you receive a fractional pension (brøkpension), e.g. 10/40.
- Foreign nationals must normally have lived in Denmark for at least 10 years to receive state pension. EU rules and social security agreements can ease this requirement, and you can also receive a pension from the other countries you have lived in.
- ATP Livslang Pension (ATP Lifelong Pension): A compulsory scheme that all employees and most benefit recipients pay into. It is paid out from the state pension age, including to people living abroad.
- Occupational pension (arbejdsmarkedspension): Agreed in the collective agreement or contract. Typically 12-18 % of pay is paid in, of which the employer pays two thirds. The scheme often includes insurance in case of death, critical illness and loss of earning capacity.
- Private pension: Voluntary savings with a bank or pension company (aldersopsparing, ratepension, livrente: age savings, instalment pension, life annuity).
- Pensionsinfo.dk gives a complete overview of all your Danish pension schemes (requires MitID).

13.8 Pension when leaving Denmark:
- Occupational pension (arbejdsmarkedspension) savings generally stay in the scheme and are paid out when you reach pension age, regardless of where you live.
- In some cases, the scheme can be paid out early (genkøb, surrender) subject to a duty of typically 60 %, or transferred to a foreign scheme. Contact your pension company.
- People on the researcher tax scheme (forskerskatteordningen, the 27 % scheme) and posted workers may have special options. Contributions to foreign pension schemes are, as a general rule, not tax-deductible in Denmark unless the scheme has been approved.

[SECTION 14: SELF-EMPLOYMENT AND FREELANCING]
Keywords: selvstændig, virksomhed, firma, CVR, enkeltmandsvirksomhed, ApS, moms, momsregistrering, freelancer, B-indkomst, faktura, virk.dk, iværksætter, self-employed, start a business, company, sole proprietorship, limited company, VAT, freelance, invoice, entrepreneur, VAT registration, B-income, business registration, bookkeeping

14.1 Starting a business:
- A business is registered digitally on virk.dk with MitID and is given a CVR number (business registration number).
- Sole proprietorship (enkeltmandsvirksomhed): The simplest form, with no capital requirement. The owner is personally liable with all their assets.
- Private limited company (anpartsselskab, ApS): Requires share capital of at least DKK 20,000. As a rule, the owner is only liable to the extent of the capital contributed.
- Public limited company (aktieselskab, A/S): Requires share capital of at least DKK 400,000.
- EU/EEA citizens can freely start a business in Denmark. Third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) must have a residence permit that gives the right to run a business, e.g. through Start-up Denmark.

14.2 VAT and tax for the self-employed:
- The business must register for VAT (moms) when turnover exceeds DKK 50,000 within 12 months. VAT is 25 % and is settled quarterly, or half-yearly for small businesses.
- Self-employed people pay their own tax in instalments (B-tax, B-skat) based on the preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) and report the year's result in the tax return (oplysningsskema) (deadline 1 July).
- The business tax scheme (virksomhedsordningen) and the capital return scheme (kapitalafkastordningen) are special tax rules that can result in lower taxation of profits that remain in the business.
- There are requirements for digital bookkeeping and for keeping accounting records for 5 years.

14.3 Freelancers and fee recipients:
- A freelancer can work as a self-employed person with a CVR number or as a fee recipient (honorarmodtager) with B-income (B-indkomst), where you pay the tax yourself.
- There are invoicing agencies (faktureringsbureauer) that, for a fee, make it possible to invoice customers without having your own CVR number.
- Self-employed people are not automatically entitled to sickness benefit (sygedagpenge) from the first day, pension or holiday pay, and should take out insurance and save up themselves.

14.4 Help for entrepreneurs:
- The Business Hubs (Erhvervshusene) and the municipal business offices offer free guidance on starting up.
- virk.dk and the Danish Business Authority (Erhvervsstyrelsen) have guides in English ("Business in Denmark").

[SECTION 15: RECOGNITION OF FOREIGN EDUCATION AND PROFESSIONAL QUALIFICATIONS]
Keywords: anerkendelse, udenlandsk uddannelse, eksamensbevis, autorisation, lovreguleret erhverv, vurdering, Uddannelses- og Forskningsstyrelsen, læge, sygeplejerske, lærer, recognition, foreign qualifications, diploma, degree, authorisation, regulated profession, assessment, foreign education, doctor, nurse, teacher, Danish Agency for Higher Education and Science

15.1 Assessment of foreign education:
- The Danish Agency for Higher Education and Science (Uddannelses- og Forskningsstyrelsen) assesses, free of charge, what a foreign qualification corresponds to in the Danish education system. The assessment can be used with employers, a-kasser (unemployment insurance funds) and educational institutions.
- The application is made digitally and requires diplomas and transcripts of grades, possibly with a translation.
- The assessment is advisory for employers but binding for, among others, a-kasser and for access to public-sector positions with educational requirements.

15.2 Regulated professions:
- A number of professions require Danish authorisation or approval before you may work. This applies to, among others, doctors, nurses, dentists, midwives, psychologists, physiotherapists, pharmacists, veterinarians, lawyers, auditors, electricians, plumbing and heating installers (vvs-installatører) and teachers in public schools (folkeskole).
- Healthcare professionals apply for authorisation from the Danish Patient Safety Authority (Styrelsen for Patientsikkerhed). For those trained outside the EU/EEA, the process is long and includes, among other things, a language test, professional tests and a period of evaluation employment (evalueringsansættelse).
- The electrical and plumbing (vvs) trades fall under the Danish Safety Technology Authority (Sikkerhedsstyrelsen).
- For EU citizens, the EU rules on mutual recognition of professional qualifications apply, which make the process faster.

15.3 Admission to further education:
- Danish educational institutions themselves assess whether a foreign exam gives admission to a specific programme, often with the help of the agency's assessment.
- There may be requirements for documented Danish or English language skills (e.g. Studieprøven i dansk (the Danish Studies Test) or an international English test).

[SECTION 16: TRANSPORT, DRIVING LICENCE, CAR AND BICYCLE]
Keywords: kørekort, ombytning af kørekort, køreprøve, bil, registreringsafgift, nummerplader, syn, bilforsikring, parkering, færdselsregler, cykel, cykelregler, offentlig transport, Rejsekort, tog, bus, metro, DSB, pendlerkort, driving licence, exchange licence, driving test, car, registration tax, licence plates, vehicle inspection, parking, traffic rules, bicycle, bike, public transport, travel card, train, commuter pass, number plates, cycling rules, car insurance

16.1 Foreign driving licence in Denmark:
- Driving licences (kørekort) from EU/EEA countries (as well as the Faroe Islands and Greenland): Valid in Denmark on the same terms as a Danish driving licence, as long as the licence is valid in the issuing country. It can voluntarily be exchanged (ombytning) for a Danish driving licence.
- Driving licences from countries outside the EU/EEA (Group 1 and Group 2 countries): Only valid in Denmark for up to 180 days (6 months) from the day you are registered as resident in the Civil Registration System (Folkeregistret). After that, the licence must be exchanged for a Danish driving licence at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service).
  * Group 1 countries (including Australia, Brazil, Canada, Japan, South Korea, New Zealand, Russia, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, Taiwan, Ukraine and the USA): The licence can be exchanged without a control driving test (kontrollerende køreprøve) (a medical certificate and a declaration of driving experience are, however, required).
  * Other countries (Group 2): You must pass a control driving test (both a theory test and a practical driving test) to have the driving licence exchanged for a Danish one.
- The list of countries changes regularly, and for some countries exchange without a test only applies to certain categories (typically an ordinary car, category B). Check the current list on borger.dk or with the Danish Road Traffic Authority (Færdselsstyrelsen).
- Tourists and people on a short stay may drive on their foreign driving licence. If the driving licence is not written in Latin letters, it must be accompanied by a translation or an international driving permit.

16.2 How to exchange a foreign driving licence:
- You must appear in person at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) (in some municipalities by appointment) and bring your original driving licence (kørekort), passport, residence documentation, a passport photo and, for countries outside the EU/EEA, a medical certificate from your own doctor.
- A fee is charged. The foreign driving licence is handed in and is normally sent back to the issuing country.
- While the case is being processed, you get a temporary driving licence, which is only valid in Denmark.
- The control driving test (kontrollerende køreprøve) can be taken in Danish or with an interpreter. In many cases the theory test can be taken in English. It is recommended to take a few driving lessons with a Danish driving instructor first.
- A Danish driving licence can also be shown digitally in the "Kørekort" app (driving licence app), which is valid in Denmark.

16.3 Getting a Danish driving licence from scratch:
- You can get a driving licence (kørekort) for a car (category B) at the age of 18, and at 17 with the requirement of an accompanying driver.
- The training includes theory lessons, driving lessons, a manoeuvring track, a skid pan and a traffic-related first-aid course, and typically costs DKK 15,000-20,000.

16.4 Importing a foreign car:
- If you move to Denmark and bring a car with foreign number plates, the car must be re-registered with Danish number plates (nummerplader), and Danish registration tax (registreringsafgift) must be paid to the Danish Motor Vehicle Agency (Motorstyrelsen) no later than 30 days after entry/address registration. Danish registration tax is high and is calculated on the basis of the car's market value in Denmark.
- The registration tax is progressive and, for petrol and diesel cars, can exceed the car's value abroad. Electric and hydrogen cars have a lower tax, which is being phased in gradually.
- The process: The car undergoes a customs inspection (toldsyn) at a vehicle inspection centre, the value is determined (by yourself or by Motorstyrelsen), the tax is paid, and the car gets Danish number plates and is registered in the Motor Register (Motorregistret).
- The exemption for household goods (flyttegods) applies to customs duty and VAT (moms) when moving from countries outside the EU, but not to the registration tax.
- Temporary stay: People who will only stay in Denmark for a limited period (typically under 185 days), or who have an employer-paid car from abroad, can in certain cases get permission to drive on foreign plates or pay a proportional registration tax. You must apply to Motorstyrelsen.
- It is a criminal offence for people resident in Denmark to drive a foreign-registered car without permission, and it can result in both a fine and a demand for the full tax.

16.5 Owning a car in Denmark:
- Liability insurance (ansvarsforsikring) is mandatory. In addition, a periodic tax (the green ownership tax, grøn ejerafgift / CO2-based tax) is paid to the Danish Motor Vehicle Agency (Motorstyrelsen).
- Passenger cars must undergo periodic vehicle inspection (syn) at fixed intervals once they have reached a certain age.
- Cars are expensive in Denmark because of the registration tax (registreringsafgift). Many people choose a used car, private leasing, car sharing or no car at all in the larger cities.
- When buying a used car, you should check the car's history, debt and inspection reports (e.g. via the Motor Register (Motorregistret) and tinglysning.dk). A change of ownership is registered digitally on motorregister.skat.dk with MitID.

16.6 The most important traffic rules:
- Traffic drives on the right. General speed limits are 50 km/h in built-up areas, 80 km/h on country roads and 130 km/h on motorways (110 km/h on certain stretches). Local limits are shown on signs.
- Dipped headlights must be on around the clock. Seat belts are mandatory in all seats. Children under 135 cm must use an approved child seat.
- The blood alcohol limit is 0.5 per mille. Drink-driving is punished severely with an income-based fine, disqualification from driving (loss of the driving licence) and, in serious cases, confiscation of the car.
- Using a hand-held mobile phone while driving is prohibited and results in a fine and a penalty point (klip) on your driving licence.
- Reckless driving (vanvidskørsel) (e.g. more than 100 % over the limit or above 200 km/h) leads to confiscation of the car, even if it is borrowed or leased.
- When turning right, drivers must give way to cyclists and pedestrians going straight ahead.
- Winter tyres are not mandatory, but are recommended from November to March.

16.7 Parking and toll roads:
- A parking disc (P-skive) is mandatory in the car's windscreen where parking is time-limited.
- In the cities there are paid parking zones, where you pay via an app (e.g. EasyPark) or a ticket machine. Residents can buy a resident's parking permit (beboerlicens) from the municipality.
- Private car parks have their own rules, and control fees (kontrolafgifter) are high (typically DKK 800-1,100).
- The Great Belt Bridge (Storebæltsbroen) and the Øresund Bridge (Øresundsbroen) charge tolls. There is no general road toll for passenger cars.
- Low-emission zones (miljøzoner) in the largest cities impose requirements on older diesel vehicles.

16.8 Bicycle:
- The bicycle (cykel) is a main means of transport in Danish towns and cities, and there are cycle paths in most places.
- Rules: Ride on the right-hand side of the cycle path, signal with your arm when turning and stopping, use lights (white at the front, red at the back) from sunset to sunrise, and do not cycle on the pavement or on pedestrian crossings. The bicycle must have a bell, reflectors and two working brakes.
- Left turns are made in two stages: Cross the junction to the opposite corner, and wait for green.
- Fines for cyclists are typically DKK 700-1,000, e.g. for riding without lights, through a red light or with a hand-held mobile phone.
- Cycle helmets are not mandatory, but are recommended.
- Bicycle theft is widespread. Use an approved lock, note down the frame number, and when buying a used bicycle, check that the frame number is not registered as stolen with the police.
- E-bikes (up to 25 km/h) follow the same rules as ordinary bicycles. E-scooters have their own rules, including compulsory helmets and mandatory insurance.

16.9 Public transport:
- Rejsekort is used for trains, the metro, light rail and buses throughout Denmark. The physical Rejsekort card is being phased out and replaced by Rejsekort as an app, where you check in and out on your phone. Tickets can also be bought in the DSB app and in the apps of the regional transport companies.
- It is important to check out at the end of your journey, as otherwise you pay a surcharge.
- The penalty fare (kontrolafgift) for travelling without a valid ticket is high (typically DKK 750-1,000).
- Commuter pass (pendlerkort): A period pass for fixed routes for commuters. The youth card (Ungdomskort) gives students and young people cheap transport between home and their place of education.
- Children under 12 often travel free with an adult, and there are discounts for young people, pensioners and for off-peak travel.
- Copenhagen has a metro (running 24 hours a day), S-trains (S-tog) and harbour buses. Aarhus and Odense have light rail. DSB operates most long-distance and regional trains; Arriva and others operate certain regional routes.
- Rejseplanen (app and rejseplanen.dk) brings together all departures and prices for public transport across the country.
- Long-distance coaches are a cheap alternative between the different parts of the country.

16.10 Travel to and from Denmark:
- Copenhagen Airport (Kastrup) is the largest airport. Billund, Aalborg and Aarhus also have international routes.
- There are direct trains to Sweden and Germany and ferries to Norway, Sweden, Germany and Bornholm, among other destinations.

[SECTION 17: DANISH LANGUAGE EDUCATION (DANSKUDDANNELSE) AND DANISH TESTS]
Keywords: dansk, danskundervisning, sprogskole, danskuddannelse, DU1, DU2, DU3, Prøve i Dansk, PD3, Studieprøven, modultest, depositum, lære dansk, Danish lessons, language school, Danish course, learn Danish, Danish test, language exam, free Danish classes, Danish language education, module test, citizenship test

17.1 Right to free Danish lessons:
- Almost all adult foreign nationals over 18 with a residence permit or EU registration certificate and a CPR number are entitled to free Danish lessons (Danish Language Education 1, 2 or 3, in Danish "Danskuddannelse 1, 2 eller 3", depending on educational background) through their municipality of residence for up to 5 years.
- For self-supporting newcomers (employees, students, EU citizens etc.), a statutory deposit (depositum) (typically DKK 2,000) is charged when the lessons start. It is repaid if you pass the module tests within the set deadlines.
- The entitlement covers approx. 3.5 years of teaching, which must be used within a period of 5 years from the time you were first able to start.
- Cross-border commuters who work in Denmark without living here are also entitled to Danish lessons.
- You enrol via the municipality or directly with a language school (sprogskole) that has an agreement with the municipality. The language school places the student at the right level after an interview.

17.2 The three Danish language programmes:
- Danish Language Education 1 (Danskuddannelse 1, DU1): For people with no schooling or very short schooling, or who cannot read and write the Latin alphabet. Concludes with Danish Test 1 (Prøve i Dansk 1).
- Danish Language Education 2 (Danskuddannelse 2, DU2): For people with a short or medium-length school and educational background. Concludes with Danish Test 2 (Prøve i Dansk 2).
- Danish Language Education 3 (Danskuddannelse 3, DU3): For people with medium-length or long education (e.g. upper secondary school or university). Concludes with Danish Test 3 (Prøve i Dansk 3) after module 5 and possibly Studieprøven (the Danish test for higher education) after module 6.
- Each programme is divided into modules, each of which concludes with a module test (modultest) before you move on.
- Lessons are offered as day classes, evening classes, weekend classes and online.

17.3 Danish tests and what they are used for:
- Danish Test 1 (Prøve i Dansk 1) (A2 level): Used, among other things, in certain family reunification cases.
- Danish Test 2 (Prøve i Dansk 2) (B1 level): Requirement for a permanent residence permit.
- Danish Test 3 (Prøve i Dansk 3) (B2 level): Requirement for Danish citizenship and a supplementary condition for faster permanent residence. Gives access to many vocational and professional programmes.
- Studieprøven (C1 level): Gives access to higher education taught in Danish.
- Danish tests A1 and A2 (Danskprøve A1 og A2): Special tests for spouses granted spousal reunification, which must be passed within a deadline after arrival.
- The citizenship tests (indfødsretsprøven and medborgerskabsprøven) are tests of Danish society, history and culture and are held twice a year. They are not language tests, but they are conducted in Danish.
- The tests are typically held twice a year (May/June and November/December) at the language schools. You can sit a test as a self-study candidate for a fee.

17.4 Tips for learning Danish:
- Many Danes quickly switch to English. Ask them to keep speaking Danish.
- Libraries have language cafés where volunteers speak Danish with newcomers, and they lend out materials free of charge.
- Associations, voluntary work and children's day-care facilities are good places to practise the language.
- Even though you can get by in English in the larger cities and at many workplaces, the vast majority of jobs, contact with the authorities and social settings require Danish in the longer term.

[SECTION 18: FAMILY, CHILDREN, DAY-CARE AND SCHOOL]
Keywords: børn, familie, vuggestue, dagpleje, børnehave, daginstitution, pladsanvisning, pasningsgaranti, friplads, skole, folkeskole, privatskole, international skole, modtageklasse, SFO, gymnasium, børnepenge, børne- og ungeydelse, børnetilskud, children, family, nursery, daycare, kindergarten, childcare, school, public school, private school, international school, after-school care, high school, child benefit, family allowance, childminder, child allowance, after-school club, reception class

18.1 Day-care facilities (nursery/vuggestue, childminder/dagpleje and kindergarten/børnehave):
- Children from approx. 6-10 months to 2 years and 10 months can be put on the waiting list for a nursery (vuggestue) or childminder (dagpleje) via Digital Pladsanvisning (the municipal childcare place allocation) on borger.dk (requires a CPR number and MitID).
- Children from approx. 3 years until they start school (the year the child turns 6) attend kindergarten (børnehave).
- Municipalities have a childcare guarantee (pasningsgaranti), but there may be waiting lists for specific facilities, so you should put your child's name down as soon as possible after CPR registration.
- A nursery is a facility with trained pedagogues (early childhood educators). Childminding (dagpleje) is the care of a small group of children in the childminder's private home.
- There are also private day-care facilities and private childcare schemes with municipal subsidies.
- Opening hours are typically from approx. 6:30-7:00 to 16:30-17:00 on weekdays.

18.2 Childcare fees and the reduced-fee subsidy (økonomisk friplads):
- Parents pay at most 25 % of the cost of the childcare place (dagtilbud); the municipality pays the rest. The price varies between municipalities and is typically approx. DKK 3,000-4,500 per month for a nursery and approx. DKK 1,800-2,800 for kindergarten, often including lunch.
- Sibling discount (søskenderabat): A 50 % discount is given on the cheapest place when a family has several children in childcare.
- Reduced-fee subsidy (økonomisk fripladstilskud): Families with a low household income can get a full or partial free place. You apply digitally to the municipality.
- The month of July is often free of charge.

18.3 Childcare and daily life:
- The children are outdoors in all kinds of weather and need practical clothing: rainwear, wellington boots, a snowsuit and a change of clothes.
- Most nursery (vuggestue) children have their midday nap outdoors in a pram or cot.
- Parents receive daily messages via an app (often Aula or a similar platform).
- Settling in (indkøring): New children start with short days together with a parent for one to two weeks.

18.4 Public school (folkeskole):
- In Denmark there are 10 years of compulsory education (undervisningspligt) (from the pre-school class/grade 0 (børnehaveklasse/0. klasse) up to and including grade 9).
- The public school (folkeskole) is free. You enrol your child by contacting the local catchment-area school (distriktsskole) or the municipality's school administration. For children who do not yet speak Danish, municipalities offer reception classes (modtageklasser) or language support.
- There is compulsory education, but not compulsory school attendance. Children can therefore attend a private school or be home-schooled under municipal supervision.
- The child belongs to the catchment-area school near their home, but parents can apply for another school if there are places available (free choice of school).
- Grade 10 is a voluntary extra year. Many young people take grade 8, 9 or 10 at an efterskole (boarding school).
- The school day typically lasts from 8:00 to 14:00-15:00. Marks are given from grade 8 on the 7-point grading scale (-3, 00, 02, 4, 7, 10, 12), and grade 9 concludes with the public school leaving examinations (folkeskolens afgangsprøver).
- Aula is the digital platform used by schools and day-care facilities for messages, timetables and contact between school and home (requires MitID).
- Parents are expected to take part in parents' meetings, parent-teacher meetings (skole-hjem-samtaler) and social events for the class.

18.5 After-school club (SFO) and leisure activities:
- An after-school club (SFO, skolefritidsordning) or after-school centre (fritidshjem) offers care before and after school hours for children in grade 0 to approx. grade 3, for a parental fee.
- Older children can attend a leisure club (fritidsklub) or youth club (ungdomsklub).
- Music schools, sports clubs and scouts are popular leisure activities with low membership fees.

18.6 Private schools and international schools:
- Independent primary and lower secondary schools (frie grundskoler: privatskoler and friskoler) receive state subsidies and charge parental fees, typically approx. DKK 1,000-3,000 per month.
- International schools teach in English, German or French according to international curricula (e.g. IB or Cambridge). They are mainly found in Copenhagen, Aarhus, Odense, Aalborg, Esbjerg and the Billund area. School fees are higher, and there are often waiting lists.
- Some municipal public schools (folkeskoler) have international departments with English-language teaching.

18.7 Youth education (upper secondary education):
- After grade 9 or 10, young people choose a general upper secondary programme (gymnasial uddannelse: STX, HHX, HTX or the 2-year HF) or a vocational education and training programme (EUD/EUX).
- The International Baccalaureate (IB) is offered in English at a number of upper secondary schools (gymnasier).
- Youth education programmes are free. Young people over 18 can receive SU (the Danish state educational grant).
- The municipality's youth guidance service (ungevejledning) helps young people under 25 choose an education.

18.8 Child benefit (børne- og ungeydelse, "børnepenge"):
- Paid quarterly (for children aged 0-14) or monthly (for young people aged 15-17) to parents who are fully liable to tax in Denmark.
- Foreign nationals earn the right to the full benefit gradually over 6 years of residence/employment in Denmark (the accrual principle, optjeningsprincippet), unless they are covered by the EU rules on the coordination of social security, under which insurance periods from other EU/EEA countries can be counted (requires an application to Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark)).
- Rates (approx. 2025 level): approx. DKK 5,100 per quarter for 0-2-year-olds, approx. DKK 4,050 for 3-6-year-olds, approx. DKK 3,200 for 7-14-year-olds and approx. DKK 1,060 per month for 15-17-year-olds.
- The benefit is tax-free, is as a rule split equally between parents with joint parental responsibility (custody) and is reduced for parents with a very high income.
- Children born in Denmark normally receive the benefit automatically. Newcomers and EU citizens who want to use periods from abroad must apply to Udbetaling Danmark themselves.
- Conditions: The child must live in Denmark (EU citizens may be entitled for children in another EU country), must not be married and must not be supported by the public authorities.

18.9 Other family benefits:
- Child allowance (børnetilskud): Extra allowance for single parents, parents in education and in the case of multiple births.
- Child maintenance (børnebidrag): The parent the child does not live with pays maintenance. The Agency of Family Law (Familieretshuset) sets the amount, and Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark) can pay out the maintenance in advance.
- A subsidy for looking after your own children and leisure passes (fritidspas) for children in low-income families are available in some municipalities.

18.10 Children's rights and upbringing:
- It is forbidden to hit children in Denmark (the right to use corporal punishment, revselsesretten, has been abolished), including for parents.
- Professionals such as pedagogues, teachers and doctors have an enhanced duty to notify (skærpet underretningspligt) the municipality if they are concerned about a child's well-being.
- Children are brought up to be independent and to have a say, and the tone between children and adults is informal.
- Børnetelefonen (the children's helpline, 116 111) is a free and anonymous counselling service for children.

[SECTION 19: MARRIAGE, COHABITATION, BIRTH, NAMES, DIVORCE AND DEATH]
Keywords: ægteskab, vielse, bryllup, prøvelsesattest, Familieretshuset, samlever, skilsmisse, separation, forældremyndighed, fødsel, fødselsregistrering, navngivning, faderskab, dødsfald, arv, testamente, marriage, wedding, certificate of marital status, cohabitation, divorce, custody, birth registration, naming, paternity, death, inheritance, will, certificate of no impediment, parental responsibility, estate, funeral

19.1 Marriage in Denmark:
- To get married, the couple must have a certificate of no impediment to marriage (prøvelsesattest), which confirms that the conditions for marriage are met. If both are Danish citizens or have permanent residence/an EU right of residence, you apply to the municipality. In other cases, the Agency of Family Law (Familieretshuset) processes the case for a fee (approx. DKK 2,000).
- The wedding (vielse) can be civil (at the town hall or another location) or a church/religious ceremony in an approved religious community.
- Same-sex couples can marry on an equal footing with other couples.
- Foreign marriage certificates are normally recognised if the marriage was validly contracted in the country where it took place. The certificate may have to be legalised or apostilled.
- Sham marriages (proformaægteskaber) entered into to obtain a residence permit do not give a right of residence.

19.2 Unmarried cohabiting couples:
- Unmarried cohabiting partners (samlevende) do not automatically inherit from each other and do not automatically have a right to each other's pension. This requires a will and beneficiary designations (begunstigelseserklæringer).
- In immigration cases, a stable cohabitation (samliv) normally lasting at least 1.5-2 years can be treated as equivalent to marriage.

19.3 Birth and registration of a child:
- The midwife notifies the authorities of the birth, and the child automatically gets a CPR number (personal identification number).
- The child must be given a name no later than 6 months after the birth. Naming is done digitally on borger.dk or at baptism in the Folkekirken (the Church of Denmark). As a rule, the first name must be an approved name; you can apply to have other names approved.
- Birth registration is handled by the parishes of the Folkekirken (in Southern Jutland (Sønderjylland) by the municipality), regardless of the parents' religion.
- Paternity/co-maternity (faderskab/medmoderskab): If the parents are married, the spouse is automatically registered as a parent. If they are unmarried, they submit a declaration of care and responsibility (omsorgs- og ansvarserklæring) digitally no later than 28 days after the birth and thereby obtain joint parental responsibility (custody).
- The child's citizenship follows that of the parents. Foreign parents must register the child themselves and apply for a passport at their home country's embassy.
- Third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) must apply for a residence permit for a child born in Denmark.

19.4 Separation, divorce and parental responsibility:
- Applications for separation or divorce (skilsmisse) are submitted digitally to the Agency of Family Law (Familieretshuset) for a fee. If the parties agree, the divorce can be granted administratively. If they have children together under 18, there is a reflection period and a mandatory digital programme.
- Joint parental responsibility (fælles forældremyndighed) is the starting point and continues after a divorce. Disagreements about the child's residence and contact (samvær) are handled by the Agency of Family Law and possibly the family court (familieretten).
- For accompanying spouses and spouses granted family reunification, a divorce may mean that their legal basis for residence lapses.

19.5 Death and inheritance:
- A death is reported to the parish (the burial authority), normally via the undertaker, within 2 working days.
- The probate court (Skifteretten) summons the next of kin and administers the estate (bobehandling).
- Under Danish law, a spouse and children are compulsory heirs (tvangsarvinger). Foreign nationals living in Denmark should consider which country's inheritance rules apply to them and possibly make a will.
- Funeral assistance (begravelseshjælp) can be applied for from the municipality, depending on the deceased's assets.

[SECTION 20: STUDENTS, HIGHER EDUCATION AND SU]
Keywords: studerende, universitet, uddannelse, optagelse, kvote 1, kvote 2, studieafgift, tuition, SU, SU-lån, studiejob, studiebolig, kollegium, udvekslingsstuderende, ph.d., student, university, higher education, admission, tuition fee, student grant, student loan, student job, student housing, dormitory, exchange student, PhD, Danish state educational grant, grading scale, halls of residence

20.1 The Danish higher education system:
- Academy profession programmes (erhvervsakademiuddannelser, approx. 2 years), professional bachelor's programmes (professionsbacheloruddannelser, 3-4 years, e.g. nurse, teacher, pedagogue), university bachelor's degrees (3 years), master's degrees (kandidat, typically 2 years) and PhDs (ph.d., 3 years).
- The grading scale is the 7-point grading scale (7-trinsskalaen): 12, 10, 7, 4, 02 (pass), 00 and -3 (fail).
- Many master's programmes are offered in English, while most bachelor's programmes are in Danish.

20.2 Admission:
- Applications for bachelor's and professional bachelor's programmes are made via optagelse.dk. The deadline is 15 March at 12 noon for quota 2 (kvote 2) and for applicants with a foreign qualifying examination, and 5 July at 12 noon for quota 1 (kvote 1).
- Applications for master's programmes are made directly to the educational institution, often with a deadline in January-March.
- There may be a requirement for Danish at the level of Studieprøven or for documented English proficiency.

20.3 Tuition fees:
- Higher education is free for Danish citizens, EU/EEA citizens and Swiss citizens, as well as for people with a permanent residence permit and certain other legal bases for residence.
- Other third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) pay tuition fees (studieafgift), typically approx. DKK 45,000-120,000 per year depending on the programme. A few scholarships are available.
- PhD students are usually employed with a salary and are regarded as employees.

20.4 SU (the Danish state educational grant, Statens Uddannelsesstøtte) for foreign nationals:
- As a rule, SU is for Danish citizens. Foreign nationals can get SU if they are granted equal status with Danish citizens under Danish rules or EU law.
- Equal status under Danish rules: E.g. if you came to Denmark with your parents before the age of 20, have been married to a Danish citizen for at least 2 years, or have lived and worked in Denmark for an extended period before your studies.
- Equal status under EU law: EU/EEA citizens who have status as a worker or self-employed person in Denmark. In practice, work alongside your studies of at least 10-12 hours a week throughout the entire grant period is required, and the requirement is checked on an ongoing basis.
- SU for students in higher education who live away from home is approx. DKK 7,000-7,500 per month before tax (2025-26 level). It can be supplemented with an SU loan (SU-lån).
- SU is applied for on su.dk with MitID and paid in advance into your NemKonto. There is a ceiling on how much you may earn on the side (fribeløb, the earnings limit).

20.5 Practical matters for international students:
- Student housing: Apply for a hall of residence (kollegium) or youth housing (ungdomsbolig) via the city's joint waiting lists as soon as you have been admitted. The educational institution's International Office can help exchange students with housing.
- Student jobs are common, typically 10-15 hours a week. Third-country nationals must comply with the limit on working hours in their residence permit.
- Student cards give discounts in many places, and the youth card (Ungdomskortet) gives cheap public transport.
- Students are covered by the public health insurance when they are registered as resident (address registration).
- Exchange students who stay for less than 3 months do not normally get a CPR number.

[SECTION 21: EVERYDAY LIFE, CULTURE AND PRACTICAL MATTERS]
Keywords: hverdag, kultur, indkøb, supermarked, åbningstider, priser, leveomkostninger, bibliotek, foreninger, fritid, sport, religion, kirke, kirkeskat, medier, vejr, tøj, hygge, omgangsformer, everyday life, culture, shopping, supermarket, opening hours, cost of living, library, clubs, leisure, sports, religion, church tax, media, weather, social norms, making friends, consumer rights, traditions, alcohol

21.1 Shopping and opening hours:
- Supermarkets are typically open every day of the week from 7:00-8:00 to 21:00-22:00. Discount chains are the cheapest; kiosks and convenience stores are the most expensive.
- Specialist shops and department stores have shorter opening hours and close earlier on Saturdays and Sundays.
- Pharmacies (apoteker) are normally open during the daytime on weekdays and on Saturday mornings. There are duty pharmacies (vagtapoteker) with long opening hours in the larger cities.
- Public offices (Borgerservice, the municipality) have short opening hours and often require an appointment.
- Second-hand items are bought and sold via e.g. DBA, Facebook Marketplace, second-hand shops and flea markets.

21.2 Cost of living:
- Denmark is one of the most expensive countries in Europe. Rent (husleje) is the biggest expense, especially in Copenhagen and Aarhus.
- Eating out, alcohol in bars and restaurants, cars, transport and tradespeople's services are expensive. Groceries cost more than the EU average.
- On the other hand, doctors, hospitals, school and education are paid for through taxes.
- A rough benchmark for a single person in Copenhagen is DKK 12,000-18,000 per month for rent, food, transport and fixed expenses, depending on the home.

21.3 Consumer rights:
- Right to complain (reklamationsret): You have a 2-year right to complain about faulty goods bought from a business.
- Right of withdrawal (fortrydelsesret): When buying online, you normally have a 14-day right of withdrawal.
- In physical shops there is no statutory right of withdrawal, but many shops voluntarily offer exchanges or returns with a receipt.
- Complaints can be brought before Center for Klageløsning (the Centre for Complaint Resolution) and Forbrugerklagenævnet (the Consumer Complaints Board) or an industry complaints board. Forbruger Europa (European Consumer Centre Denmark) helps with purchases in other EU countries.

21.4 Libraries and community centres:
- Public libraries (folkebiblioteker) are free and lend out books, films, games and e-books (eReolen). A library card is created with your health card (sundhedskort) or MitID.
- Libraries have computers, printing, study spaces, language cafés, homework help and help with digital self-service solutions.
- Many libraries have self-service opening hours from early morning to late evening.

21.5 Associations and leisure:
- Denmark has a very extensive associational life (foreningsliv). Sports clubs, evening schools, choirs, scouts and special-interest associations are the easiest way to meet Danes.
- Membership fees are low because the associations are run by volunteers and receive municipal support.
- Evening schools (aftenskoler, e.g. FOF, AOF and LOF) offer courses in everything from languages to cooking and ceramics.
- Voluntary work is a good way into networks and the language. Third-country nationals should make sure that unpaid voluntary work is compatible with their residence permit.
- International networks exist in the larger cities, including through International House, expat associations and the municipalities' welcome events.

21.6 Social conventions:
- Danes are informal and use first names and the informal "du" (you) with everyone, including bosses, teachers and doctors.
- Punctuality is important, both for meetings and for private arrangements.
- Privacy is respected, and Danes may seem reserved towards strangers. Friendships usually develop through shared activities over time.
- Visits are arranged in advance. You typically take your shoes off in private homes and bring a small gift for the host.
- "Hygge" refers to relaxed time together, often at home, with food, candles and plenty of time.
- Gender equality is a fundamental norm, and both parents typically take part in childcare and housework.
- Trust is a hallmark of Danish society, e.g. unattended roadside stalls and prams left outside cafés.

21.7 Traditions and special days:
- Christmas is celebrated on the evening of 24 December. December is marked by Christmas lunches (julefrokoster) and Advent traditions.
- Other traditions include Shrovetide (fastelavn), Easter, Midsummer (sankthans, 23 June with bonfires), St Martin's Eve (mortensaften, 10 November) and New Year's Eve with the King's New Year speech and fireworks.
- Birthdays are celebrated with the Danish flag (Dannebrog), which is used on all festive occasions.
- Confirmation and the upper secondary school leaving examination (studentereksamen, with the student cap and the student truck ride) are major events for young people.

21.8 Religion and the Church of Denmark (Folkekirken):
- There is freedom of religion in Denmark. The Evangelical Lutheran Church of Denmark (Folkekirken) has a special status and is the largest church.
- Only members of the Folkekirken pay church tax (kirkeskat). Newcomers do not automatically become members, but can join. Members of equivalent Lutheran churches in other Nordic countries should check their registration.
- You leave the Folkekirken by contacting the parish.
- There are mosques, Catholic churches, synagogues, temples and many other religious communities, especially in the larger cities.

21.9 Alcohol, smoking and drugs:
- Strong alcohol may only be sold to people over 18. Young people aged 16-17 may only buy drinks with a low alcohol content, and the rules have been tightened in recent years. Shops may require photo ID.
- Serving alcohol in bars and restaurants requires the guest to have turned 18.
- Tobacco and nicotine products may only be sold to people over 18, and prices are high. Smoking is prohibited indoors at workplaces, on public transport, at schools and in most bars, cafés and restaurants.
- All narcotic drugs, including cannabis, are illegal to possess, buy and sell.

21.10 Weather and clothing:
- The climate is a temperate coastal climate with mild, windy winters (approx. 0-5 degrees) and cool summers (approx. 17-25 degrees). It rains all year round.
- Winter days are short and dark (approx. 7 hours of daylight in December), and summer days are very long (approx. 17 hours in June). Many people take vitamin D in winter.
- Practical rainwear and layered clothing are recommended, especially if you cycle.
- Denmark uses summer time (daylight saving time), and the time zone is Central European Time (CET).

21.11 Media and news:
- DR and TV 2 are the largest public service media. The major daily newspapers have paywalls.
- News about Denmark in English is available from The Copenhagen Post and The Local Denmark, among others.
- Warnings about serious incidents are issued via sirens and the mobile alert system (S!RENEN). The sirens are tested every year on the first Wednesday in May at 12 noon.
- The authorities recommend that all households be able to manage on their own for 3 days with water, food, medicine and other necessities in a crisis.

21.12 Democracy and society:
- Denmark is a constitutional monarchy with a parliament (Folketinget) of 179 members. The government is led by the prime minister.
- The country is divided into 98 municipalities and 5 regions. The municipalities handle most local services for citizens (schools, childcare, elderly care, Borgerservice, job centre).
- The Danish Realm (Rigsfællesskabet) also includes the Faroe Islands and Greenland, which have self-government and are not members of the EU. A residence permit for Denmark does not automatically apply there.
- Denmark is a member of the EU, but has kept the krone and has opt-outs in areas including justice and home affairs.

[SECTION 22: RIGHTS, ELECTIONS, POLICE, ID AND LEGAL AID]
Keywords: rettigheder, stemmeret, valg, kommunalvalg, politi, anmeldelse, 112, 114, straffeattest, børneattest, legitimation, ID-kort, pas, diskrimination, retshjælp, advokat, klage, ombudsmand, GDPR, rights, voting, elections, police, report crime, criminal record, ID card, passport, discrimination, legal aid, lawyer, complaint, data protection, right to vote, child protection certificate, emergency number, equal treatment

22.1 Voting rights and elections:
- General (parliamentary) elections (folketingsvalg) and referendums: Only Danish citizens over 18 who are resident in the Realm can vote.
- Municipal and regional elections (kommunalvalg and regionsvalg, held every four years in November): Citizens of EU countries, Iceland and Norway can vote and stand as candidates once they have turned 18 and are resident in the municipality. Other foreign nationals can vote after 4 years of continuous residence in the Realm.
- European Parliament elections: EU citizens resident in Denmark can choose to vote in Denmark instead of in their home country.
- The polling card (valgkort) is sent automatically to your address. You can cast a postal vote (brevstemme) at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) in the weeks before the election.

22.2 Police and emergencies:
- 112: Emergency number for immediate danger to life, health or property (police, ambulance, fire).
- 114: The police service number for non-urgent enquiries.
- Theft, vandalism and fraud can be reported online at politi.dk with MitID (the national digital ID). Insurance companies normally require a police report.
- Trust in the police is high, and contact is informal. Bribery is a criminal offence and practically never occurs.
- Domestic violence: Lev Uden Vold's (Live Without Violence) 24-hour hotline (1888) gives anonymous advice. Special rules apply to spouses who came through family reunification (familiesammenføring) and are subjected to violence, so that the residence permit is not necessarily lost when the couple stop living together.

22.3 ID (legitimation):
- Denmark does not have a national ID card that is valid as a travel document. Danish citizens use a passport when travelling.
- Foreign nationals should be able to show their passport and residence card/registration certificate if checked, and must carry them when crossing a border.
- ID card (legitimationskort): For a fee, the municipality issues a photo ID card to people over 15 who are resident in the municipality. It can be used as proof of age and photo ID in Denmark, but not for travel.
- Driving licences and health cards are often used as ID in everyday life. The health card (sundhedskort) has no photo and is not valid photo ID on its own.

22.4 Criminal record certificate and child protection certificate:
- A private criminal record certificate (privat straffeattest) can be ordered free of charge on politi.dk with MitID and is sent to Digital Post. Employers often ask for it when hiring.
- A child protection certificate (børneattest) is obtained by employers and associations when you are going to work with children under 15, and it requires your consent.
- Newcomers may be asked for a criminal record certificate from their home country.
- For foreign nationals, criminal offences can lead to expulsion (udvisning) and block permanent residence and citizenship, even in minor cases.

22.5 Discrimination and equal treatment:
- Discrimination on grounds of gender, race, skin colour, religion, political opinion, sexual orientation, gender identity, age, disability and national, social or ethnic origin is prohibited in the labour market. Outside the labour market, discrimination on grounds of gender, race, ethnic origin and disability, among others, is prohibited.
- The Board of Equal Treatment (Ligebehandlingsnævnet) handles complaints free of charge. The Danish Institute for Human Rights (Institut for Menneskerettigheder) gives advice on discrimination.
- Hate crimes should be reported to the police.

22.6 Legal aid and lawyers:
- Free legal aid (retshjælp) is offered by legal aid offices (retshjælpskontorer) and lawyers' advice services (advokatvagter, often at libraries), where you can get oral advice anonymously.
- Legal expenses insurance (retshjælpsforsikring) is part of most contents insurance policies (indboforsikring) and can cover lawyers' fees in civil cases.
- People on a low income can get free legal proceedings (fri proces) for court cases.
- If you are arrested, you have the right to a defence lawyer and an interpreter.

22.7 Complaints about authorities:
- Decisions from public authorities must include guidance on how to appeal (klagevejledning), stating the deadline and the appeals body.
- Immigration cases are typically appealed to the Immigration Appeals Board (Udlændingenævnet), social cases to the National Social Appeals Board (Ankestyrelsen) and tax cases to the Danish Tax Appeals Agency (Skatteankestyrelsen).
- The Parliamentary Ombudsman (Folketingets Ombudsmand) can handle complaints about public authorities once the other avenues of complaint have been exhausted.
- You have the right of access to the documents in your own case (aktindsigt) and the right to be represented by someone else (partsrepræsentant, a party representative).
- The Danish Data Protection Agency (Datatilsynet) handles complaints about the processing of personal data (GDPR).

[SECTION 23: PETS, CUSTOMS AND HOUSEHOLD GOODS]
Keywords: kæledyr, hund, kat, EU-pas til kæledyr, rabies, mikrochip, hunderegister, forbudte hunderacer, told, flyttegods, indbo, toldfrihed, moms ved import, medicin, kontanter, pets, dog, cat, pet passport, microchip, banned dog breeds, customs, household goods, removal goods, duty free, import VAT, cash declaration, ferret, rabies vaccination, dog register

23.1 Entering Denmark with a dog, cat or ferret:
- From other EU countries: The animal must be identified with a microchip, be vaccinated against rabies (at least 21 days before entry in the case of a first vaccination) and have an EU pet passport (EU-selskabsdyrspas) issued by a vet.
- From countries outside the EU: A microchip, rabies vaccination and an animal health certificate are required. From certain countries, a blood test (antibody test) taken well before the journey is also required, and entry must take place via an approved point of entry.
- A maximum of 5 animals can be brought in as part of private travel. Puppies and kittens under approx. 15 weeks can normally not be brought in.
- The rules are administered by the Danish Veterinary and Food Administration (Fødevarestyrelsen).

23.2 Rules for dogs in Denmark:
- All dogs must be microchipped and registered in the Danish Dog Register (Dansk Hunderegister). Dogs brought into the country must be registered shortly after arrival.
- Liability insurance (ansvarsforsikring) for dogs is required by law.
- Dogs must be kept on a lead in towns and built-up areas unless they are under full control, and always on a lead in forests and on beaches in the summer half of the year (with local exceptions).
- It is prohibited to keep or import 13 dog breeds and crosses of them: pit bull terrier, tosa inu, American Staffordshire terrier, fila brasileiro, dogo argentino, American bulldog, boerboel, kangal, Central Asian ovtcharka, Caucasian ovtcharka, South Russian ovtcharka, tornjak and sarplaninac.
- Many rented homes do not allow pets. Check the tenancy agreement (lejekontrakt) and the house rules (husorden).
- Cats should be identified and registered. Vet visits are expensive, and pet health insurance is common.

23.3 Household goods (flyttegods) from countries outside the EU:
- Used household effects and personal belongings can be imported free of customs duty and VAT as household goods (flyttegods) if you have lived outside the EU for at least 12 consecutive months, have owned and used the items for at least 6 months, and import them within 12 months of the move.
- The household goods must be declared to customs, normally via the removal company or freight forwarder, with a list of the contents and documentation of the move.
- The items must not be sold, rented out or transferred during the first year after import.
- Alcohol, tobacco and business equipment are not covered by the exemption.
- A car you bring with you may be exempt from customs duty and VAT as household goods, but Danish registration tax (registreringsafgift) must still be paid.

23.4 Moving within the EU:
- There is no customs duty or VAT on private belongings moved between EU countries.
- Cars must, however, be re-registered, and registration tax (registreringsafgift) must be paid. New vehicles may trigger Danish VAT (moms).

23.5 Goods with special rules:
- Cash: Amounts of EUR 10,000 or more must be declared to the Danish Customs Agency (Toldstyrelsen) when entering or leaving the EU.
- Medicine for personal use can be brought in limited quantities; for narcotic medicines (euforiserende lægemidler) a special certificate is required.
- Weapons, knives, pepper spray and fireworks are strictly regulated. Pepper spray and most knives in public places are illegal without a legitimate purpose.
- Meat and dairy products must not be brought in from countries outside the EU. Plants and seeds may require a phytosanitary certificate.

[SECTION 24: LEAVING DENMARK]
Keywords: fraflytning, flytte fra Danmark, udrejse, afmelde CPR, afmelding, skat ved fraflytning, feriepenge ved fraflytning, pension ved fraflytning, opsige lejemål, leaving Denmark, moving abroad, deregister, departure, exit, final tax, holiday pay payout, pension refund, terminate lease, emigrating, exit tax

24.1 Checklist when leaving Denmark:
- Terminate your lease with the agreed notice (typically 3 months), and arrange a move-out inspection (fraflytningssyn).
- Report your departure (udrejse) to the municipality on borger.dk (no earlier than 4 weeks before you leave) if your stay abroad will last more than 6 months. Provide your new address abroad.
- Notify the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) and correct your preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) so that your tax liability ends correctly.
- Keep MitID and Digital Post. Both work from abroad and are needed for your annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse), holiday pay and pension.
- Keep a Danish bank account as your NemKonto for a period, or register a foreign account as your NemKonto.
- Request payment of your holiday pay (feriepenge) from FerieKonto.
- Contact your pension company about the options for your accumulated pension savings.
- Cancel subscriptions (electricity, internet, mobile, insurance, gym, media) and notify your bank of your change of address.
- Ask your a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) for a PD U1 as documentation of insurance periods for use in another EU country.
- Ask for a statement/reference from your employer, your diplomas and documentation of your children's schooling.
- Hand in your residence card if the authorities ask you to, and deregister your car and number plates or export the car (you can apply for an export refund of the registration tax, eksportgodtgørelse).

24.2 Tax when leaving Denmark:
- Full tax liability normally ends when you give up your home in Denmark and move. If you keep a home available to you in Denmark, full tax liability may continue.
- You receive an annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse) for the part of the year in which you were liable to tax, in March of the following year. Any tax refund (overskydende skat) is paid to your NemKonto.
- Income from Denmark after the move (e.g. renting out a Danish home, a Danish pension, board fees) may still be taxable in Denmark under limited tax liability (begrænset skattepligt).
- If you hold large shareholdings, exit tax (fraflytterskat) may be calculated.

24.3 Health and social benefits when leaving Denmark:
- The right to the yellow health card (sundhedskort) and a Danish doctor ends when your departure is registered.
- Child benefit (børne- og ungeydelse), housing benefit (boligstøtte) and most other residence-based benefits stop. Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark) must be notified.
- Earned entitlement to state pension (folkepension) and ATP is not lost for EU citizens and citizens of countries with a social security agreement, but it is only paid out at pension age.

24.4 Residence permit when leaving Denmark:
- A residence permit normally lapses when you give up your residence in Denmark or have stayed abroad for more than 6 months (12 months after more than 2 years of lawful residence). This also applies to a permanent residence permit.
- You can apply in advance to the Danish Immigration Service (Udlændingestyrelsen) for an exemption (dispensation) so that the permit does not lapse during a temporary stay abroad (e.g. a posting or education).

[SECTION 27: TYPICAL PROBLEMS AND SOLUTIONS FOR NEWCOMERS]
Keywords: problem, fejl, afslag, sidder fast, virker ikke, kan ikke få, hjælp, ventetid, forsinkelse, hønen og ægget, troubleshooting, stuck, rejected, not working, cannot get, delay, catch-22, help, problems, solutions, refusal

27.1 Problem: I cannot get a CPR number because I do not have an address, and I cannot rent a home without a CPR number.
- Solution: A tenancy agreement (lejekontrakt) does not legally require a CPR number (personal identification number). Explain to the landlord that the CPR number will come once you have moved in, and show your passport and employment contract instead. Alternatively, you can stay temporarily with a friend or colleague or in a sublet room, where the host signs a host declaration (logiværtserklæring). Never choose a home where you are not allowed to register your address.

27.2 Problem: I cannot get a bank account without a CPR number, and my employer wants to pay my salary.
- Solution: The employer can temporarily pay the salary into a foreign account, or hold back the payment for a short time until the account is ready. Start the bank's account-opening process as soon as you have received your CPR number, and ask for a basic payment account (basal betalingskonto) if the bank refuses a regular customer relationship.

27.3 Problem: 55 % tax has been deducted from my first salary.
- Solution: This is because your employer has not been able to retrieve a tax card. Apply for a tax card (skattekort) on skat.dk. Once the card is registered, the correct withholding rate (trækprocent) will be used from then on. The overpaid tax is not lost: the employer can often adjust it in the next payroll run, and otherwise the amount is paid out as a tax refund (overskydende skat) after the annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse).

27.4 Problem: My health card (sundhedskort) has not arrived.
- Solution: Check that your name is on the letterbox and that the address (including floor and side, e.g. "2. tv.") is registered correctly. Contact Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) if the card has not arrived after 3-4 weeks. In the meantime, use the "Sundhedskortet" app or the letter from the municipality showing your CPR number and doctor.

27.5 Problem: The MitID app cannot scan my passport.
- Solution: Not all countries' passports are supported, and the phone must have NFC. Book an appointment at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) to set up MitID in person, and bring your passport, residence card/registration certificate and, if possible, a witness who has MitID.

27.6 Problem: I have no one in Denmark who can act as a witness for MitID.
- Solution: Ask a colleague, fellow student, landlord or mentor. Without a witness, Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) can in some cases confirm your identity based on the biometrics in your residence case or by asking control questions. Call Borgerservice in advance and ask what they can accept.

27.7 Problem: The municipality refuses to register my address.
- Solution: A refusal is typically due to missing documentation of housing, lack of a legal basis for residence (opholdsgrundlag), or the stay being too short. Ask for a written explanation. Obtain a tenancy agreement (lejekontrakt) or a host declaration (logiværtserklæring), and make sure the landlord does not have more people registered at the address than the home is approved for.

27.8 Problem: My documents (marriage certificate, birth certificate) are not being accepted.
- Solution: Documents from many countries must be legalised or given an apostille in the issuing country and translated by a certified translator. Without approved documentation, your marital status is registered as unknown, but the CPR registration itself can normally be completed. The documents can be submitted later.

27.9 Problem: I am not receiving post from the public authorities.
- Solution: All post from public authorities comes in Digital Post and not in your physical letterbox. Log in to borger.dk, e-Boks or mit.dk with MitID, and sign up for email and text message reminders.

27.10 Problem: My residence permit expires soon, and I have not had an answer about the extension.
- Solution: If you applied for an extension before the permit expired, you normally have the right to stay in Denmark and continue working until a decision has been made. Keep the receipt for your application. If you travel abroad, you may need a re-entry permit (tilbagerejsetilladelse) from SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration) or the Danish Immigration Service (Udlændingestyrelsen).

27.11 Problem: I have lost my job and have a work permit that is tied to the job.
- Solution: Notify SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration). Under several schemes, you can get a job-seeking residence period (jobsøgningsophold) of up to 6 months. A new job requires a new application for a work permit. Talk to your a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) and trade union (fagforening) about the terms of your notice and unemployment benefits (dagpenge).

27.12 Problem: My landlord will not return my deposit (depositum).
- Solution: Ask for a written statement with documentation of the costs. Compare it with the move-in report and your own photos. Bring the case before the Rent Tribunal (huslejenævnet) (or the Residents' Complaints Board, beboerklagenævnet, in public non-profit housing), possibly with help from a tenants' association.

27.13 Problem: I do not understand a letter from an authority.
- Solution: Call the authority; most can give guidance in English. Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service), ICS (International Citizen Service), the library and volunteer advice services can also help. Always respond before the deadline in the letter, as failing to respond can lead to refusal, loss of benefits or fees.

27.14 Problem: I have received a text message or email with a link about tax, a parcel or MitID.
- Solution: It is probably fraud (phishing). Authorities and banks do not send links where you have to enter your MitID or card details. Instead, log in yourself via the official website or app. If you have clicked and approved, you must immediately block your cards and MitID and contact your bank.

[SECTION 28: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS (FAQ) FOR THE RAG SYSTEM]
Keywords: FAQ, spørgsmål og svar, ofte stillede spørgsmål, hvordan, hvornår, hvor lang tid, hvad koster, kan jeg, skal jeg, questions and answers, how do I, when, how long, how much, can I, do I need, frequently asked questions, how much does it cost

28.1 FAQ ON RESIDENCE AND CPR

Question: How long does it take to get a CPR number?
Answer: If all your documentation is in order (passport, EU registration certificate/residence permit, tenancy agreement and any civil status documents), your CPR number (personal identification number) is usually created immediately when you appear in person at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) or International Citizen Service (ICS). The physical health card (sundhedskort) itself arrives by post 2-3 weeks later.

Question: What is the difference between Borgerservice and ICS (International Citizen Service)?
Answer: Borgerservice is the municipality's local citizen service office for all citizens. ICS (International Citizen Service) is a specialised collaboration between SIRI (the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration), the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) and the municipalities of Copenhagen, Aarhus, Odense and Aalborg, where foreign workers and students can sort out their residence papers, CPR number and tax card in one and the same visit.

Question: Can I live in a hotel or an Airbnb and get a CPR number?
Answer: As a general rule, no. Temporary stays in a hotel, hostel or holiday home/Airbnb do not normally give the right to address registration (bopælsregistrering) in the Civil Registration System (Folkeregistret), unless the stay is of such a duration and permanent nature that it constitutes your actual permanent residence, and the landlord/hotel confirms this in writing to the municipality.

Question: Do I need an EU registration certificate before I can get a CPR number?
Answer: Yes, if you are a citizen of an EU/EEA country or Switzerland and are going to live in Denmark for more than 3 months. You first apply for the EU registration certificate (EU-registreringsbevis) from SIRI and then take the certificate with you to Borgerservice. At ICS both can be done in the same visit. Nordic citizens do not need a registration certificate.

Question: I am a Nordic citizen. What do I need to do to move to Denmark?
Answer: You do not need a visa, residence permit or registration certificate. If you are going to live in Denmark for more than 6 months, you must register your move with the municipality at Borgerservice, bringing ID and documentation of your housing. Provide your personal identity number from your previous Nordic country of residence.

Question: Can I get a CPR number if I am going to be in Denmark for less than 3 months?
Answer: No, not an ordinary CPR number with address registration. If you work in Denmark, you will receive a tax personal number (skattepersonnummer, an administrative CPR number) from the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) when you apply for a tax card.

Question: How much does it cost to get a CPR number?
Answer: It is free to register your address and get a CPR number and health card. The EU registration certificate is also free. Residence and work permits for third-country nationals (non-EU/EEA citizens) are subject to a fee.

Question: Do my children have to appear in person to get a CPR number?
Answer: Yes. All family members who are to be registered must appear in person at Borgerservice or ICS with their passport and birth certificate.

Question: What is a host declaration (logiværtserklæring)?
Answer: A host declaration (logiværtserklæring) is a written confirmation from the person you live with (the owner or tenant of the home) that you actually live at the address. It is used as documentation of housing when you are not named on a tenancy agreement yourself.

Question: When must I report my move if I move to a new address in Denmark?
Answer: No later than 5 days after the move and no earlier than 4 weeks before. You report your move digitally on borger.dk with MitID.

Question: Do I lose my CPR number if I move away from Denmark?
Answer: No. Your CPR number is yours for life. You are registered as having left the country and lose your health card and your own doctor (GP), but the number is reactivated if you move back.

Question: Can I apply for a work permit after entering Denmark as a tourist?
Answer: The general rule is that third-country nationals must apply for and obtain the permit before they enter Denmark. People with lawful residence in Denmark can in some cases submit the application here, but they must not start working until the permit has been granted. Check the conditions for the specific scheme on nyidanmark.dk.

Question: Is my spouse allowed to work in Denmark?
Answer: Accompanying spouses of foreign workers and of most students with a residence permit may work freely without their own work permit. Spouses of EU citizens have the right to work under EU rules. Spouses of Danish citizens who have been granted family reunification may also work.

Question: When can I get a permanent residence permit?
Answer: Third-country nationals can normally apply after 8 years of lawful residence, or after 4 years if all the supplementary conditions are met. The requirements include, among other things, having passed Danish Test 2 (Prøve i Dansk 2), employment, self-sufficiency and no serious crime. EU citizens gain the right to permanent residence (tidsubegrænset ophold) after 5 years of lawful residence under EU rules.

Question: Can I have dual citizenship in Denmark?
Answer: Yes. Denmark allows dual citizenship. However, you should check whether your country of origin allows it.

28.2 FAQ ON HEALTH

Question: Are doctor's visits free in Denmark?
Answer: Yes. Visits to a general practitioner (GP) (praktiserende læge), to a specialist (speciallæge) with a referral, and treatment at public hospitals are free when you have a health card (sundhedskort). You pay yourself for the dentist (adults), part of the cost of medicine, psychologists and physiotherapy.

Question: What do I do if I fall ill in the evening or at the weekend?
Answer: Call the out-of-hours medical service (lægevagt) or the emergency medical helpline (akuttelefon) in your region: 1813 in the Capital Region of Denmark (Region Hovedstaden), 1818 in Region Zealand (Region Sjælland). You must call before you turn up at an emergency department. If life is in danger, call 112.

Question: Can I see a doctor before I have received my health card?
Answer: Yes, once you have registered your address and received a CPR number, you are covered. Use the "Sundhedskortet" app or the letter from the municipality. Before you have a CPR number, you are only entitled to emergency treatment and should have private insurance or a European Health Insurance Card (EU-sygesikringskort) from your home country.

Question: How do I change doctor?
Answer: You change doctor on borger.dk with MitID for a fee. If you move to a new address, you can choose a new doctor free of charge as part of your change-of-address notification.

Question: Does the yellow health card cover me when I travel abroad?
Answer: No. When travelling in the EU/EEA and Switzerland, you use the blue European Health Insurance Card (EU-sygesikringskort), which you order free of charge on borger.dk. In addition, private travel insurance that also covers transport home is recommended.

Question: Do I have to pay for the dentist?
Answer: Adults pay for the dentist themselves, with a small public subsidy that is deducted from the bill. Children and young people get free dental care through the municipality.

Question: Can I get an English-speaking doctor?
Answer: Most doctors in Denmark speak English. You cannot choose a doctor by language in the system, but you can call the clinic and ask before you choose. If needed, the doctor will book an interpreter.

28.3 FAQ ON MITID AND DIGITAL POST

Question: Can I get MitID without a Danish passport?
Answer: Yes. If you have a foreign biometric passport (with a chip) that is supported by the MitID app, you can scan the passport in the app and set up MitID (the national digital ID) yourself. If your passport does not work in the app, you can have MitID set up by appearing in person at Borgerservice with your passport and a witness (vitterlighedsvidne: a person over 18 who has had MitID for at least 30 days), or via the police's/SIRI's biometric register.

Question: Can I get MitID without a CPR number?
Answer: In practice, you need a CPR number or an administrative CPR number to get a MitID that can be used for public services and online banking.

Question: What do I do if I lose my phone with the MitID app?
Answer: Block the MitID app immediately on mitid.dk or by calling MitID support on 33 98 00 10. Then install the app on a new phone and activate it by scanning your passport, using a code display (kodeviser) or going to Borgerservice in person.

Question: Where do I find post from the municipality, Skat and the hospital?
Answer: In your Digital Post, which you read on borger.dk, mit.dk or e-boks.dk, or in the associated apps, with MitID. Physical letters are not normally sent.

Question: What happens if I do not read my Digital Post?
Answer: The letters have the same legal effect as paper letters. Deadlines run even if you have not opened the letter, and you may miss appointment notices, decisions and bills. Sign up for email or text message reminders.

28.4 FAQ ON TAX

Question: Do I have to pay tax in Denmark from day one?
Answer: Yes, if you work in Denmark, tax must be deducted from your salary from your first working day. Remember to apply for a tax card (skattekort) from the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen, skat.dk) as soon as you have received your CPR number (or tax personal number, skattepersonnummer), so that your employer does not deduct 55 % in tax.

Question: How much tax do people pay in Denmark?
Answer: An ordinary employee pays around 35-40 % of their salary in tax in total, including the 8 % labour market contribution (AM-bidrag). The exact rate depends on income, municipality and deductions. The highest incomes have a marginal tax rate of approx. 56-60 %.

Question: What is the difference between the preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) and the annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse)?
Answer: The preliminary income assessment is a budget for the current year, which determines your withholding rate (trækprocent) and your deductions. The annual tax assessment is the final statement for the previous year, which arrives in March and shows whether you will get money back or have to pay back tax (restskat).

Question: Do I have to hand in my tax card to my employer?
Answer: No. The tax card (skattekort) is digital, and your employer retrieves it directly from the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) using your CPR number.

Question: Can I apply for a tax card before I have a CPR number?
Answer: Yes. You can fill in the application for a tax card and personal number (form 04.063) on skat.dk without a CPR number and MitID. The Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen) will then assign you a tax personal number (skattepersonnummer).

Question: Do I have to pay Danish tax on income and savings in my home country?
Answer: If you are fully tax liable in Denmark, you must declare your entire global income, including interest, dividends, rental income and pension from abroad. Double taxation agreements ensure that you do not pay full tax twice.

Question: What is the researcher tax scheme (forskerskatteordningen)?
Answer: A special scheme for highly paid employees and researchers recruited from abroad. The salary is taxed at 27 % plus the labour market contribution (32.84 % in total) for up to 7 years. There is a requirement for a high minimum salary and that you have not been tax liable in Denmark in the last 10 years. The employer applies for the scheme.

Question: When do I get a tax refund?
Answer: Overpaid tax (overskydende skat) appears on the annual tax assessment (årsopgørelse) in March and is paid out automatically to your NemKonto, typically in April.

Question: Do I have to pay church tax (kirkeskat)?
Answer: Only if you are a member of the Church of Denmark (Folkekirken). Newcomers are not automatically members.

28.5 FAQ ON BANKING AND MONEY

Question: Can I get a Danish bank account before I have a CPR number?
Answer: In practice, no. Almost all Danish banks require a valid Danish CPR number and a registered Danish address to open an account. A few banks can start the process with an administrative CPR number (tax personal number, skattepersonnummer) for cross-border commuters.

Question: What is a NemKonto?
Answer: A NemKonto is the bank account you have designated to receive all payments from the public authorities, e.g. tax refunds, SU (the Danish state educational grant), child benefit and housing benefit (boligstøtte). It is an ordinary account and not a special type of account.

Question: The bank will not open an account for me. What can I do?
Answer: Explicitly ask for a basic payment account (basal betalingskonto), which everyone with lawful residence in the EU is entitled to. The bank must give you a written, reasoned refusal, which you can complain about. Also try another bank.

Question: Can my salary be paid into a foreign account?
Answer: Yes, many employers can do this, and a foreign account can be registered as your NemKonto. However, third-country nationals on certain work-based schemes must have their salary paid into a Danish bank account.

Question: How do I get MobilePay?
Answer: You need a CPR number, MitID, a Danish mobile number and an account with a Danish bank. Then you download the app and sign up.

28.6 FAQ ON HOUSING

Question: How much do I have to pay when moving into a rented home?
Answer: The landlord may require up to 3 months' rent as a deposit (depositum) and up to 3 months' prepaid rent (forudbetalt leje). Together with the first month's rent, the total can be up to 7 months' rent.

Question: What happens if I do not put my name on the letterbox?
Answer: The postal worker is not allowed to deliver post to you if your name is not on the letterbox. This means that important letters such as your yellow health card, bank cards and PIN codes are returned to the sender, which delays your whole start in Denmark.

Question: How long is the notice period for a rented home?
Answer: As a tenant, you can normally give notice on a flat with 3 months' notice and on a single room with 1 month's notice. Check your tenancy agreement.

Question: How do I avoid housing scams?
Answer: Never pay a deposit before you have seen the home and signed a contract. Only pay by bank transfer to a Danish account. Be suspicious if the landlord is abroad, the price is very low, or you are not allowed to register your address.

Question: Can I get housing benefit (boligstøtte)?
Answer: If you rent a home with its own kitchen and have a low or moderate income, you can apply for housing benefit from Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark) via borger.dk. The amount depends on the rent, the size of the home, the household's income and the number of people.

Question: Am I allowed to buy a home in Denmark as a foreigner?
Answer: EU/EEA citizens can buy a year-round home for their own use without permission. Third-country nationals without 5 years of previous residence normally need permission from the Danish Civil Affairs Agency (Civilstyrelsen), which is typically granted when you have a residence permit and will live in the home yourself. Summer houses are subject to special, strict rules.

Question: How do I get electricity in my new home?
Answer: You must choose and sign up with an electricity supplier yourself when you move in. Compare prices on elpris.dk.

28.7 FAQ ON WORK

Question: Is there a minimum wage in Denmark?
Answer: There is no statutory minimum wage. Pay is set in collective agreements (overenskomster) between trade unions and employers or in the individual contract.

Question: Do I have to be a member of a trade union or an a-kasse?
Answer: No, both are voluntary. An a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) gives you the right to unemployment benefits (dagpenge) if you become unemployed, and a trade union (fagforening) helps with pay, contracts and disputes. Many people are members of both.

Question: How much holiday am I entitled to?
Answer: All employees accrue 2.08 days of holiday per month, corresponding to 5 weeks of holiday per year. Many have a 6th week of holiday through a collective agreement or their contract.

Question: What happens to my holiday pay if I leave Denmark?
Answer: Accrued holiday pay (feriepenge) is paid into FerieKonto when you leave your job. When you move away from Denmark, you can have it paid out by requesting this on borger.dk.

Question: How long is parental leave (barsel) in Denmark?
Answer: Together, the parents are entitled to 48 weeks of parental leave benefit (barselsdagpenge) after the birth, as a starting point 24 weeks each. For employees, 11 weeks are earmarked for each parent. The mother also has 4 weeks of leave before the birth.

Question: Can I get unemployment benefits (dagpenge) if I lose my job?
Answer: Only if you have been a member of an a-kasse (unemployment insurance fund) for at least 1 year and meet the income requirement. EU citizens can transfer insurance periods from their home country with the PD U1 form if they join a Danish a-kasse within 8 weeks.

Question: Can I work in Denmark without speaking Danish?
Answer: Yes, especially in international companies, IT, research, engineering, hotels and restaurants, cleaning, agriculture and construction. However, Danish significantly increases your job opportunities and is necessary in most public-sector jobs and customer-facing positions.

Question: What are normal working hours in Denmark?
Answer: A full-time week is normally 37 hours. Average working hours must not exceed 48 hours per week including overtime.

28.8 FAQ ON DRIVING LICENCES, CARS AND TRANSPORT

Question: Can I drive on my foreign driving licence in Denmark?
Answer: Driving licences (kørekort) from EU/EEA countries can be used without time limit as long as they are valid. Driving licences from other countries may be used for up to 180 days after you have taken up residence in Denmark, and must then be exchanged for a Danish driving licence.

Question: Do I need to take a driving test to exchange my driving licence?
Answer: It depends on the issuing country. Driving licences from the EU/EEA and a number of other countries (including the USA, Canada, Australia, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and Ukraine) are exchanged without a test. Driving licences from other countries require a control theory test and practical driving test.

Question: Can I drive my foreign car when I live in Denmark?
Answer: No, not permanently. Once you are resident in Denmark, the car must be registered in Denmark and registration tax (registreringsafgift) must be paid within 30 days, unless you have received a special permit from the Danish Motor Vehicle Agency (Motorstyrelsen).

Question: How do I pay for buses and trains?
Answer: With Rejsekort (as an app), in the DSB app or in the regional transport companies' apps. Remember to check in and out. Commuters can buy a commuter pass (pendlerkort).

28.9 FAQ ON LANGUAGE, CHILDREN AND EDUCATION

Question: Are Danish lessons (danskundervisning) free?
Answer: Yes, for most newly arrived adults with a CPR number. Self-supporting participants pay a deposit of typically DKK 2,000, which is refunded when the modules are passed on time.

Question: When can my child start at nursery (vuggestue) or kindergarten (børnehave)?
Answer: You can put your child on the waiting list via Digital Pladsanvisning on borger.dk as soon as the child has a CPR number. The municipality has a childcare guarantee (pasningsgaranti) and must offer a place within a set deadline, but not necessarily at the facility you want.

Question: Does it cost anything to go to school in Denmark?
Answer: The public school (folkeskole) is free. Private schools and international schools charge school fees. Upper secondary education (ungdomsuddannelser) and higher education are free for EU/EEA citizens and persons with permanent residence.

Question: My child does not speak Danish. How does starting school work?
Answer: The municipality assesses the child's needs and offers a reception class (modtageklasse) or language support in a regular class (Danish as a second language). Contact the municipality's schools department or the local catchment-area school (distriktsskole).

Question: Can I get child benefit (børnepenge) as a newcomer?
Answer: You earn the right gradually over 6 years of residence or employment in Denmark. EU citizens can have periods from other EU countries counted and must apply to Udbetaling Danmark (Payout Denmark).

Question: Can foreign students get SU?
Answer: EU/EEA citizens can get SU (the Danish state educational grant) if they have worker status in Denmark, which in practice means working at least 10-12 hours a week alongside their studies. Other foreign nationals can get SU if they meet special conditions for equal status with Danish citizens.

28.10 FAQ ON LEAVING DENMARK AND OTHER TOPICS

Question: What do I need to do when I move away from Denmark?
Answer: Give notice on your home, report your departure (udrejse) to the municipality on borger.dk, notify the Danish Tax Agency (Skattestyrelsen), have your holiday pay paid out, contact your pension company, and keep MitID and Digital Post so that you can receive your annual tax assessment and other letters.

Question: Can I bring my dog or cat to Denmark?
Answer: Yes. The animal must have a microchip, a valid rabies vaccination and an EU pet passport (from the EU) or an animal health certificate (from countries outside the EU). 13 dog breeds are banned in Denmark. Dogs must be registered in the Danish Dog Register (Dansk Hunderegister) and have liability insurance.

Question: Can I vote in Denmark?
Answer: EU citizens as well as Norwegian and Icelandic citizens can vote in municipal and regional elections (kommunal- og regionalvalg) when they are resident in Denmark. Other foreign nationals can vote in these elections after 4 years of residence. Only Danish citizens can vote in general (parliamentary) elections (folketingsvalg).

Question: Which insurance policies do I need?
Answer: Most people have at least contents insurance (indboforsikring) with liability insurance (ansvarsforsikring). In addition, accident insurance and travel insurance are common. Car owners and dog owners must have statutory liability insurance.

Question: Who do I call in an emergency?
Answer: Call 112 in case of immediate danger to life or property. Call 114 for the police when it is not an emergency. Call the out-of-hours medical service (lægevagt) or the emergency medical helpline (akuttelefon) (e.g. 1813 in the Capital Region of Denmark) in case of acute illness outside your doctor's opening hours.

[SECTION 29: QUICK REFERENCE - KEY QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS]
Keywords: moving to Denmark, CPR number, residence permit, EU registration certificate, MitID, health card, yellow card, tax card, bank account, NemKonto, Digital Post, housing, deposit, Danish lessons, driving licence, child benefit, unemployment insurance

This section summarises the most important points of the knowledge base as short questions and answers. The sections above contain the full details.

29.1 What is the correct order of steps when moving to Denmark?
1) Secure your legal basis for residence (EU registration certificate from SIRI for EU/EEA/Swiss citizens, residence and work permit for non-EU citizens, nothing for Nordic citizens). 2) Find a place to live where you are allowed to register your address. 3) Register at Borgerservice (the municipal citizen service) or International Citizen Service (ICS) to get a CPR number. 4) Choose a GP and receive the yellow health card by post. 5) Set up MitID, the national digital ID. 6) Apply for a tax card and fill in the preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) at skat.dk. 7) Open a Danish bank account and designate it as your NemKonto. 8) Activate Digital Post (e-Boks, mit.dk or borger.dk).

29.2 What is a CPR number and who needs one?
The CPR number is a ten-digit civil registration number (DDMMYY-XXXX). Anyone staying in Denmark for more than three months (six months for Nordic citizens) must register their address and will get a CPR number. It is required for health care, salary, banking, tax and almost all contact with the authorities. Registration is free and done in person.

29.3 What documents do I need to bring to get a CPR number?
Passport or national ID card, EU registration certificate or residence permit (not required for Nordic citizens), proof of address in Denmark (tenancy agreement or a declaration from the person you live with), marriage certificate if married, and birth certificates for accompanying children. Documents not in Danish, English, German, Norwegian or Swedish normally need a certified translation and possibly legalisation or an apostille.

29.4 Can I register for a CPR number while staying in a hotel or Airbnb?
Normally not. You need a permanent place to live where you actually stay. A PO box or a c/o address without real residence is not accepted.

29.5 How do EU/EEA citizens get the right to stay?
EU/EEA and Swiss citizens can stay for up to three months without registration (six months as jobseekers). For longer stays they must apply to SIRI for an EU registration certificate as a worker, self-employed person, student, person with sufficient funds, or family member. The certificate is free and must be issued before the municipality can give a CPR number.

29.6 How do non-EU citizens get the right to live and work in Denmark?
They normally need a residence and work permit before arrival, applied for through nyidanmark.dk (New to Denmark). Common schemes are the Pay Limit Scheme, the Positive Lists, the Fast-track Scheme for certified companies, the researcher scheme, study permits and family reunification. A fee applies and biometrics (photo and fingerprints) must be recorded.

29.7 How do I get MitID as a foreigner?
You need a CPR number. If you have a biometric passport supported by the MitID app and a smartphone with NFC, you can scan your passport and face in the app. Otherwise book an appointment at Borgerservice and bring your passport and, if possible, a witness who has had MitID for at least 30 days.

29.8 How does health care work?
Once registered with a CPR number you are covered by public health insurance. GP visits and hospital treatment are free. You choose a GP when you register, and the GP refers you to specialists. Adults pay for dental care and part of the cost of prescription medicine. Call 112 in life-threatening emergencies; call the regional out-of-hours medical service (1813 in the Capital Region) before going to an emergency department.

29.9 How does tax work for newcomers?
You pay tax from your first working day. Apply for a tax card at skat.dk; without one your employer must withhold 55 %. Your tax card is electronic and your employer retrieves it automatically. The preliminary income assessment is your tax budget for the year, and the annual tax assessment in March shows whether you get a refund or owe tax. Total tax for an average employee is roughly 35-40 %, including the 8 % labour market contribution. Highly paid employees and researchers recruited from abroad may qualify for the special 27 % expat tax scheme for up to seven years.

29.10 How do I open a bank account?
Banks normally require a CPR number, passport, Danish address, proof of employment or study, and your foreign tax identification number. Everyone legally residing in the EU has the right to a basic payment account. Tell your bank to register the account as your NemKonto so that public authorities can pay money to you.

29.11 What should I know about renting?
A landlord may ask for up to three months' rent as a deposit plus up to three months' prepaid rent. Report defects in writing within 14 days of moving in. Tenants can normally terminate with three months' notice. Put your full name on the mailbox, otherwise your health card and bank cards will not be delivered. Never pay a deposit before you have seen the home and signed a contract.

29.12 Is there a minimum wage?
There is no statutory minimum wage. Pay and conditions are set by collective agreements between trade unions and employers or by the individual contract. All employees are entitled to five weeks of paid holiday per year.

29.13 What are an a-kasse and a trade union?
An a-kasse is a voluntary unemployment insurance fund; normally one year of membership plus an income requirement gives the right to unemployment benefits. A trade union gives legal help and advice on contracts and pay. EU citizens can transfer insurance periods from another EU country with form PD U1 if they join a Danish a-kasse within eight weeks.

29.14 Can I use my foreign driving licence?
Licences from EU/EEA countries are valid in Denmark. Licences from other countries can be used for 180 days after you register your address and must then be exchanged; depending on the issuing country, a theory and practical test may be required. A car brought from abroad must be registered in Denmark and registration tax paid within 30 days.

29.15 Are Danish lessons free?
Yes, most adult newcomers with a CPR number are entitled to free Danish courses through their municipality. Self-supporting students pay a refundable deposit of about DKK 2,000.

29.16 What about children?
Sign children up for nursery or kindergarten through the digital waiting list on borger.dk; municipalities guarantee a place. Public school is free and compulsory education lasts ten years. Child benefit is earned gradually over six years of residence or employment, but EU rules allow periods from other EU countries to count.

29.17 What should I do when leaving Denmark?
Terminate your lease, report your departure to the municipality, inform the tax agency, request payout of holiday pay, contact your pension provider, and keep your MitID and Digital Post so you can still receive your annual tax assessment.
